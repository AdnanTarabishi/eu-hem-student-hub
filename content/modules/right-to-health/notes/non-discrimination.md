## 5-minute review

- Different treatment of comparable situations can raise a discrimination issue.
- Identical treatment of relevantly different situations can also raise an issue.
- Direct discrimination expressly uses a protected ground; indirect discrimination can use a neutral-looking criterion.
- Ground and field both matter: a rule for employment is not automatically a rule for every health service.
- The short summary’s examples depend on evidence and context.
- A legitimate objective does not remove the need to examine proportionality.
- Migration-related health entitlements differ by legal status and special needs.

## A disciplined sequence for analysis

The lecture starts with comparison. Ask which people or situations are being compared, which characteristics are relevant and what treatment differs. Next identify the ground, such as nationality or sex, and the setting, such as work or access to services. Then identify the applicable instrument. A statement that something feels unfair is a starting concern, not the completed legal analysis.

The short summary identifies three paths: economic free-movement rights, non-discrimination directives and human-rights protection. They have different scopes. The Charter’s broader list of grounds does not make the field and application questions disappear.

## Direct and indirect: look beyond the wording

Direct discrimination explicitly uses the ground in the decision. Indirect discrimination may arise from a neutral-looking criterion that disadvantages a protected group. In the short summary’s staff-event example, full-time priority can raise a sex-discrimination issue if evidence shows the relevant unequal pattern of part-time work. That factual condition must not be removed from the explanation.

The language example similarly distinguishes a sufficient command of the language for the actual job from a demand for perfection that is unnecessary for its tasks. The study lesson is to examine the requirement and its effects, not to declare every language requirement unlawful.

## The field of the directive is part of the answer

The deck contrasts racial or ethnic origin under Directive 2000/43, employment and occupation under Directive 2000/78, sex equality in goods and services under Directive 2004/113, and employment equality under Directive 2006/54. Its matrix is a teaching device for seeing differences of coverage.

A blank area in that matrix should not be turned into a claim that no other legal protection exists. Nor should the student apply an employment directive to a different setting without explaining why it is relevant. The slides’ reproductive-technology examples show why the precise ground and instrument change the reasoning.

## The ground–field–instrument test

*Expanded reading*

A claim needs more precision than “two people were treated differently”. The lecture links nationality and product origin to economic freedoms, distinguishes the Treaty’s nationality provision from other protected grounds, and presents several non-discrimination directives. For each problem, write down the proposed ground, the field of activity, and the instrument being invoked. A rule about employment does not automatically govern every service, and the existence of a broad Charter list does not remove the need to establish its scope of application.

The source distinguishes racial or ethnic origin under Directive 2000/43, employment-related protection under Directive 2000/78, sex equality in goods and services under Directive 2004/113, and sex equality in employment under Directive 2006/54. It specifically identifies healthcare in the racial-equality framework. These distinctions make a useful reading map, not an exhaustive account of every applicable rule. Where a slide lists a reform or a proposal, retain that status and consult the original instrument before asserting that the entire legal framework has changed.

## Why evidence and a comparator change the answer

*Expanded reading*

The short summary’s staff-event example uses full-time work as an apparently neutral priority rule. Its explanation is conditional: evidence that the rule disproportionately affects women can support an indirect sex-discrimination analysis. The text does not say that all full-time/part-time distinctions have the same effect. Identify who is disadvantaged, why the comparison is relevant, and what information supports the claimed connection. Intent to discriminate and the effects of a rule are different evidential questions.

The language example likewise asks whether the required level is necessary for the actual job. Some command of a language may be needed; a demand for perfect fluency may be excessive for the tasks described. The summary then considers objective justification and proportionality. A useful answer tests whether the measure can achieve the legitimate aim and whether a less restrictive alternative could do so. This is an original organisation of the supplied reasoning, not a general permission to justify every form of direct discrimination on any convenient ground.

## Keep reproductive and migration examples legally distinct

*Expanded reading*

The reproductive examples show why the right claimed and the applicable definition matter. The Mayr discussion distinguishes protection under the pregnant-workers framework from possible sex-equality protection. The D and Z discussions concern the position of a commissioning mother and particular maternity-leave or equality claims. These are not presented as one general decision about whether assisted reproduction is morally acceptable. Study the issue, the relevant instrument and the limited conclusion separately; the source does not provide a current country-by-country guide to reproductive rights.

The migration section also distinguishes legal categories. The slides on beneficiaries of international protection describe healthcare under the same eligibility conditions as nationals; the reception-conditions slide separately addresses applicants, children and special reception needs. Other slides concern temporary protection, long-term residence and return. The deck’s final summary is much more compressed than these detailed provisions. Preserve both the existence of that broad summary and the specific distinctions: it would be misleading to turn the entire section into a single emergency-care entitlement for all migrants.
