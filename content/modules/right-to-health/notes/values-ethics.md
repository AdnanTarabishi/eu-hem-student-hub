## 5-minute review

- Ethics is reflective inquiry; morality includes norms of conduct shared in a social setting.
- Values are more abstract than action-guiding principles.
- Article 2 TEU values and the 2006 health-system values have different legal status.
- The health values are universality, access to good-quality care, equity and solidarity.
- Operating principles connect values to quality, safety, evidence, participation and redress.
- Personal values and values people associate with the EU are different survey constructs.
- The historical Eurobarometer figures are not current cohort or population statistics.

## Ethics, morality, values and principles

The lecture places ethics within practical philosophy and presents morality as shared norms of right and wrong conduct. Ethical reflection can evaluate a prevailing norm rather than merely repeat it. Values express broad commitments, whereas principles give more concrete guidance for decision-making and action.

The lecture’s biomedical principles include autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence and justice. Its legal and health-system principles partly overlap with ethical reasoning but are not identical to it. To say that a policy advances a value is therefore not yet to specify its legal effect or how it should be implemented.

## Two layers of values

The Article 2 TEU discussion concerns the Union’s foundational values, including dignity, freedom, democracy, equality, the rule of law and human rights. The 2006 health-system discussion identifies universality, access to good-quality care, equity and solidarity. The lecture distinguishes binding primary law from non-binding health-minister conclusions.

The Council text connects equity to access according to need and solidarity to financing arrangements that sustain accessibility. It also recognises variation between national systems. A shared value does not prescribe one identical insurance design for all countries.

## Make the value operational

The operating principles include quality and safety, evidence and ethics, patient involvement, redress, and privacy and confidentiality. They help turn a general aspiration into a question about delivery: what information will patients receive, how can they participate, how is risk monitored, and how can an error be challenged?

Original reflection prompt: a service is formally open to everyone but requires complex online registration. Which accessibility and participation questions should be asked? The sources provide the concepts; they do not provide an official answer to this invented scenario.

## Read the values diagram from abstract commitments to action

*Expanded reading*

The diagram on page 29 places common EU values and health-specific values above more concrete ethical, legal and operational principles, with trust as an overarching goal. It separately names autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence and justice, alongside legal principles such as transparency, proportionality and responsibility. This is a conceptual arrangement: it helps explain why stating a broad value is not the same as describing what a service should actually do. The diagram does not supply a numerical formula for balancing all principles.

Original application: a proposed digital appointment system could claim to promote equity, but the claim needs an operational explanation. Accessible information, a non-digital alternative and a way to challenge an error are possible design responses to analyse, not requirements asserted by the slide itself. Name the value, identify the practical principle, explain the action, and disclose a remaining conflict. The purpose is to develop reasoning that a classmate can question, rather than decorate a policy with a list of values that never influence its design.

## Ethical disagreement does not disappear at the border

*Expanded reading*

The lecture opens its ethics discussion by revisiting sensitive issues and the distribution of competence. It then uses recitals from the patient-mobility directive to show legal references to ethical choices and professional duties. The example is not that economic integration has erased all moral disagreement. Rather, the course places law and ethics in a relationship mediated by values and human rights. The same healthcare activity can therefore raise an internal-market question and an ethical question without those questions being identical.

The repetition of human obligations adds a further perspective. A rights-based statement asks what a person should receive or be allowed to do; a duty-focused statement asks who must act and how. In an original seminar answer, follow a claim such as “patients should be involved” with an account of who provides information, who makes the decision, and how the patient’s contribution can matter. The lecture provides this distinction in orientation; it does not give an official answer to every ethical controversy raised in discussion.

## Surveys, legal commitments and recommendations are different evidence

*Expanded reading*

The Eurobarometer slides distinguish values respondents personally hold from values they associate with the EU. A survey finding describes answers to a question; it is not itself the legal source of the Union’s values. The charts also use historical waves and flag the introduction of “respect for the planet” as a response category in 2019. When comparing bars, preserve the survey question, wave, population and available answer categories. Do not interpret a newly introduced category as evidence that nobody cared about it in earlier years.

The legal-status distinction is equally important. The lecture presents Article 2 TEU as binding primary law and the 2006 health-values conclusions as non-binding soft law. Soft law can still structure discussion and expectations, but its status should not be silently upgraded. A defensible paragraph can therefore distinguish three statements: what people report valuing, what a legal text establishes, and what policymakers are urged to pursue. These may support each other, but they require different evidence and do not answer the same question.
