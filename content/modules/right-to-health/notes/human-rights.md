## 5-minute review

- Human rights, fundamental rights and citizenship rights are related but not interchangeable terms.
- The EU Charter and the ECHR belong to different institutional systems.
- Article 35 concerns access to preventive care and medical treatment, with national conditions.
- AAAQ means availability, accessibility, acceptability and quality.
- Accessibility includes financial, physical, information and non-discrimination dimensions.
- Respect, protect and fulfil identify different state obligations.
- Legal permission for a vaccination policy is not the same question as whether it should be chosen.

## Which document and which court?

The lecturer distinguishes citizenship rights tied to nationality, fundamental rights by their foundational importance, and human rights held by human beings. The EU, the Council of Europe and the UN are not interchangeable sources. Name the document before interpreting the right.

The lecture pairs the EU Charter with the Court of Justice in Luxembourg and the European Convention on Human Rights with the European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg. It notes similarities in interpretation without merging the two systems. An answer that cites Article 8 ECHR should therefore not attribute that provision to the EU Charter.

## From a broad idea of health to a usable framework

The WHO material in the lecture treats health more broadly than absence of disease. General Comment 14 provides a framework for examining the services and conditions needed to realise the right. Availability asks whether sufficient functioning provision exists. Accessibility asks whether people can reach and use it without discriminatory, geographical, financial or information barriers.

Acceptability asks whether care respects ethics and cultural contexts. Quality asks whether services are scientifically and medically appropriate. These are complementary checks, not a league table. A well-equipped clinic can still be inaccessible, while a nearby affordable service can still have a quality problem.

## Who must do what?

The slides move beyond a simple positive-versus-negative rights split. Respect concerns avoiding interference with enjoyment of the right. Protect concerns preventing interference by third parties. Fulfil concerns the legislative, administrative, budgetary and other measures needed to make the right effective.

Article 35 is presented as a healthcare entitlement connected to national law and practice, not an unlimited guarantee of any desired treatment. Supplementary scope note: the Commission’s public explanation says that national authorities are bound by the Charter when implementing EU law. A study answer should state the scope question rather than imply that Article 35 decides every domestic dispute by itself.

## Široká and Padova: why the court may not reach the merits

*Expanded reading*

In Široká, the lecture describes a challenge connected with a parent’s refusal to vaccinate a child. The important lesson is jurisdiction: the Charter applies to Member States when they implement Union law, and the Court did not obtain a sufficient EU-law basis to answer the questions presented. That procedural result is not a substantive judgment that every vaccination duty is justified. First establish the legal connection; only then examine the relevant right and any interference with it.

The Padova discussion concerns a nurse suspended after refusing a national vaccination requirement. The slides stress inadequacies in the referring court’s information and the link between the questions and the dispute. They also distinguish an authorisation to market a vaccine from a legal obligation to receive it. When comparing these cases, explain the procedural issue separately from the public-health controversy. Do not combine a jurisdictional limit, an inadmissible reference and a full proportionality analysis into the single statement that the court approved mandatory vaccination.

## Vavřička: reconstruct the sequence, not just the outcome

*Expanded reading*

The Vavřička slides organise Article 8 analysis as scope, interference and justification. Physical integrity falls within private life; recognising an interference begins the analysis rather than ending it. Justification is then considered through legal accessibility and foreseeability, a legitimate aim, and necessity in a democratic society. The protection of others includes children unable to receive a vaccine themselves. The margin of appreciation concerns the space for national decision-making, but the slides also emphasise continuing judicial review.

The specific safeguards matter to that assessment: no vaccination was forcibly administered to the applicants, medical contraindications were considered, sanctions were discussed, and administrative and judicial remedies existed. The source also addresses safety monitoring and the relevance of compensation. These facts should accompany any summary of the reasoning. A different disease, population, sanction or implementation method would require its own analysis. Finally, distinguish the legal question of whether a measure may be justified from the ethical question of whether it should be the preferred policy.

## Use AAAQ alongside respect, protect and fulfil

*Expanded reading*

AAAQ describes dimensions of the service or health system: sufficient provision, practical accessibility, cultural and ethical acceptability, and scientific and medical quality. Respect, protect and fulfil instead organise the duties of the responsible authority. They are complementary frameworks, not rival lists. The lecture describes respect as refraining from interference, protection as addressing interference by others, and fulfilment as taking measures towards realisation. Do not classify a problem under one heading and assume that this already identifies every required action.

Original application: a functioning clinic may still be unaffordable, hard to reach and unable to provide information patients can use. These are different accessibility barriers, not proof that the clinic does not exist. A separate question is which actor can change fees, transport, communication or provider behaviour. This example illustrates how to link a barrier to an obligation; it is not a case from the lecturer. The broad WHO framing of health also explains why a discussion limited to the hospital building would omit social and other determinants identified in the course.
