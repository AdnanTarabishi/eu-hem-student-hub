## 5-minute review

- Follow three reform waves, while distinguishing the 1987 proposal from the 2006 insurance reform.
- Regulated competition shifts purchasing toward insurers without abandoning public access and quality goals.
- A benefits package, an insurance contract and a provider contract are different things.
- Compare in-kind delivery with reimbursement obligations; do not confuse these concepts with current product availability.
- Risk equalisation pays insurers; the income-related care allowance supports eligible households.
- Basic-insurance protections do not automatically transfer to supplementary insurance.
- For care abroad, identify the route, entitlement system, provider conditions and price reference before calculating.

## 1. Three reform waves: different problems, different instruments

*Guest lecture study guide*

The lecture organises reform into three overlapping waves: extending coverage and equal access, controlling expenditure, and introducing incentives and competition. In the Dutch account, the first wave runs roughly from 1940 to 1970, the second from 1970 to 2000, and the third from around 2000. The 1941 Sickness Fund Decision required membership for employees below an income threshold. Contributions went to a central fund, which paid sickness funds on a cost basis. The later two-tier arrangement combined public sickness-fund insurance for lower incomes with voluntary private insurance for higher incomes; the slides give approximate population shares of two thirds and one third. These are historical descriptions, not the structure of basic coverage after 2006.

From the 1970s and 1980s, price controls, capacity planning and macro-budgets became more detailed. The lecture’s explanation for reform in the mid-1980s combines cumbersome regulation, weak efficiency incentives for several actors and problems in the private-insurance market. Its list of failed national-insurance proposals includes opposition from doctors and private insurers, distributional and expenditure concerns, and political differences. Original interpretation: expanding entitlement, containing spending and creating efficiency incentives are different policy tasks. The third wave does not mean that access or regulation ceased to matter, and the 1987 proposal should not be mistaken for the 2006 implementation date.

## 2. Regulated competition: changing who purchases care

*Guest lecture study guide*

The Dekker proposals paired mandatory insurance for everyone with regulated competition among insurers and among providers. In the lecture, the purchaser role shifts from government toward competing private insurers. Government’s tools include legislation, prices, budgets and capacity planning; insurers use provider contracts, selective contracting and negotiation about price and quality. This is expressly not an unregulated market: affordability, access and quality remain public goals that regulation is meant to protect. The account also describes a long period of convergence between public and private insurance before the new national arrangement.

Read the triangle on page 27 as two distinct contractual relationships: the consumer holds an insurance policy with an insurer, while the insurer contracts with care providers. Being insured and choosing a particular provider are therefore not identical decisions. Original example: two plans may cover the same broad category of treatment but differ in their provider networks and payment arrangements. The mechanism to explain is how purchasing could reward appropriate, efficient care while rules protect access. The diagram is an institutional model; it does not prove that every insurer buys effectively or that every contract has the same terms.

## 3. Basic insurance: package, acceptance, premium and deductible

*Guest lecture study guide*

The 2006 Health Insurance Act is presented as a mandate to buy individual private basic insurance, with a standard package expressed in functions of care rather than a fixed list of provider organisations. That wording is intended to leave room for different ways of delivering covered care. Open enrolment and community rating sit alongside selective contracting and permitted vertical integration. In this teaching framework, open enrolment concerns acceptance into basic insurance; community rating concerns the premium for a given product rather than charging an individual according to their health risk. Neither term says that every policy or every insurer must have an identical premium.

The lecture states an adult mandatory deductible of €385 for 2026 and an optional increase of up to €500 per year. On those slide figures, the highest combined deductible is €885, not €500. This is an arithmetic reading of pages 17 and 21, not a complete list of exemptions or a recommendation to choose that amount. The annual choice discussed in the deck includes the insurer, product, preferred-provider arrangements and optional supplementary coverage. Separate the regular premium, deductible and any additional payment caused by a reimbursement limit: they are not interchangeable labels for one payment.

## 4. In-kind contracts: an obligation to arrange care

*Guest lecture study guide*

For an in-kind contract, the lecturer emphasises delivery of care within acceptable waiting-time norms, not merely repayment of a bill. Insurers contract with providers, including providers outside the Netherlands. Contracted care is described as received without a separate full treatment bill, subject to the deductible and, in the later cross-border slide, copayments. For non-contracted care, the insurer sets reimbursement subject to the lecture’s no-hindrance principle. The question is therefore not just whether the service is in the benefits package, but also what the insurer has promised to arrange and which provider relationship applies.

Pages 24 and 43 describe a general 70–80% reimbursement range using costs considered reasonable in the Dutch market as the reference. Do not turn this into 70–80% of any foreign invoice or a universally fixed statutory rate. The slides explicitly mention litigation and possible conditions such as a referral, insurer approval, and payment to the patient rather than directly to the provider. The contract must state the conditions. The lecture also discusses urgent care separately; page 42 adds a Dutch-cost ceiling for care abroad. A percentage alone does not settle the actual amount payable or determine the result of a dispute.

## 5. Reimbursement contracts: distinguish the model from market availability

*Guest lecture study guide*

The lecturer contrasts an in-kind delivery obligation with a reimbursement model: payment for covered care across providers, unless the price is exceptionally high relative to the Dutch market. Page 25 describes an effort to inform and assist the insured with waiting times rather than the same delivery guarantee described for in-kind care. Read the statement about going to court in that specific waiting-time context; it is not a claim that a reimbursement-policy holder can never contest any refusal or breach. Combination products are mentioned on page 21, but the slides do not specify every service-by-service term.

Supplementary verification, separate from the lecture: the NZa’s 2024–2026 policy table lists zero pure reimbursement policies in both 2025 and 2026, alongside in-kind and combination policies. The distinction in the slides remains useful for understanding promises and reimbursement rules, but should not be advertised as a current catalogue of products students can buy. The table below deliberately compares the lecture’s concepts. A real policy may mix arrangements by service, so neither the label alone nor the course summary establishes a personal entitlement or a guaranteed court outcome.

## 6. Read the funding diagram: three inflows, two different subsidies

*Guest lecture study guide*

The page-28 diagram separates income-related contributions flowing to the Risk Equalization Fund (REF), direct adult premiums flowing to insurers, and a government contribution associated with people under 18. It labels these flows 50%, 45% and 5% respectively. The fund then makes payments to insurers using risk adjusters. These percentages describe the lecture’s financing diagram; they are not percentages of each patient’s bill and should not be extrapolated to the entire Dutch health and long-term-care budget. The agenda explicitly limits the main system discussion to somatic healthcare.

Two transfers must be kept separate. Risk equalisation compensates insurers according to expected risk, whereas the income-related care allowance helps eligible households with affordability. The slide also states an allowance maximum of €2,952 per household per year in 2026 and a household coverage proportion. These are attributed slide statements, not an eligibility calculator or an independently verified distributional estimate. Original illustration: a household’s allowance and its insurer’s risk-adjusted payment can both exist, but they have different recipients, purposes and calculation bases. The diagram’s central lesson is to follow each arrow before interpreting its percentage.

## 7. Risk adjustment is not risk selection

*Guest lecture study guide*

The deck lists demographic, regional, socioeconomic and health-use adjusters: age/gender, region, income source, household information, pharmacy- and diagnosis-based groups, prior costs, medical devices, physiotherapy groups and prior home-care costs. These are inputs to compensation, not permission to reject a high-risk applicant for basic insurance. Original explanation: when otherwise similar premiums are charged to people with very different expected needs, compensation must account for those needs if insurers are to have reasons to compete for patients rather than only for favourable risk profiles.

The lecturer identifies three potential harms of risk selection: poorer responsiveness and quality for chronically ill people, diversion of effort from efficiency improvement, and segmentation that undermines solidarity. A financially attractive portfolio is not automatically evidence of better care. Conversely, the presence of many adjusters does not itself establish that all residual selection incentives have disappeared. The earlier Dutch readings provide a separate, dated discussion of this issue; they are not substituted for the guest slides. Original evaluation question: does a plan’s apparent success reflect better purchasing and care, or the kinds of patients it attracts?

## 8. Supplementary insurance: a different regulatory boundary

*Guest lecture study guide*

The lecture places supplementary insurance outside the mandatory basic basket. Its examples repeatedly qualify physiotherapy, dental care, medical devices and foreign coverage with “as far as not in the basket”. This qualification matters: the slide does not say that every instance of those services is excluded from basic insurance. Other examples include alternative medicine and a payment connected with childbirth. The analytical task is to identify the benefit and its position relative to the basic package before discussing the supplementary contract.

Page 32 attributes a view to the Dutch government: essential benefits belong in the mandatory package, while access to benefits outside it is not treated in the same way. The slide therefore describes insurers as free to define supplementary coverage, differentiate products and premiums, and use selection. Do not transfer basic-insurance open-enrolment and community-rating protections automatically to supplementary insurance. Equally, the heading “No specific regulation” should not be expanded into an unsupported assertion that no general contract or consumer law applies. The course material supports a contrast in the insurance rules discussed, not an exhaustive legal inventory.

## 9. Coordination: EHIC and S2 use the host system’s entitlements

*Guest lecture study guide*

The second half introduces Regulations 883/2004 and 987/2009 as the coordination route. Page 35 uses “urgent care” with the European Health Insurance Card and “non-urgent care” with prior approval through S2. Its main contrast is the entitlement basis: the statutory system in the country providing treatment rather than the wording of the Dutch home policy. Page 36 links S2 to treatment covered at home and to waiting times that are too long. This is a concise teaching account; it does not provide the complete assessment procedure, a numerical universal waiting threshold or a decision for a particular patient.

Supplementary clarification, not a silent rewrite of page 35: the European Commission describes EHIC coverage as medically necessary state-provided care during a temporary stay, under the host system’s conditions and costs. That is broader than emergencies alone, and it does not cover a trip undertaken expressly to obtain treatment. A useful original study question is therefore “Why was the person travelling, and which route is being used?” rather than “Is the procedure urgent?” alone. The card is not a guarantee of free services or a substitute for travel insurance. This clarification does not establish any individual student’s eligibility.

## 10. The Patients’ Directive: an additional route, not a replacement

*Guest lecture study guide*

The lecture presents Directive 2011/24/EU as developing the free-movement approach from Court of Justice cases while leaving coordination in place. It names cases including Decker, Vanbraekel, Smits, Peerbooms, Müller-Fauré and Watts. The source’s spelling “Kohl” is not used here as a case-identification test. The two routes coexist: adopting the Directive does not erase the S2 route or turn an EHIC into authorisation for planned treatment. National transposition and home-insurer conditions matter in the lecturer’s account.

Page 38 compresses the reimbursement principle and its qualifications into a short statement about financial stability, especially inpatient care. Do not treat that sentence as an exhaustive legal test or as automatic permission to refuse every inpatient claim. The adjacent slides themselves require attention to coverage, authorisation, conditions and national implementation. For course analysis, identify the route and then the relevant conditions before reaching a bounded conclusion. A complete legal determination would require the applicable rules and case facts beyond this deck; the study questions therefore test distinctions, not blanket entitlement promises.

## 11. Dutch coverage abroad: location does not remove contract limits

*Guest lecture study guide*

The Dutch implementation discussion describes worldwide coverage under mandatory insurance and says the same distinction between contracted and non-contracted providers matters at home, elsewhere in the EU and outside it. Entitlements remain tied to the Dutch policy and its type. Crucially, the lecturer caps reimbursement abroad at the amount the care would cost in the Netherlands. The urgent-care exception on page 42 must be read with that ceiling. Geographic breadth is therefore not the same as unlimited financial coverage, and the lecture’s mention of additional travel insurance is not a recommendation of a specific product.

Original arithmetic example, not an actual claim: assume an eligible foreign bill of €1,500, a Dutch reference amount of €1,000, and a fictional contract paying 75% of that reference for non-contracted care. Before deductibles, copayments or other conditions, the illustration gives €750 reimbursement and a €750 remainder. It does not give €1,125 by multiplying the foreign bill by 75%. This example illustrates the importance of the calculation base, not the precise calculation in every policy. Even a 100% reference-based limit need not cover a foreign invoice above that reference.

## 12. Build a cross-border answer without mixing routes

*Guest lecture study guide*

Page 45 draws the lecture together: within the EU it contrasts coordination, using EHIC or S2 as appropriate, with entitlements based on the Dutch policy; outside the EU it discusses the Dutch worldwide-coverage contract route. Use the table as a map of the lecture, not an exhaustive country-by-country eligibility guide. The slide’s reference to a Dutch citizen should not be used by itself as proof that nationality creates insurance entitlement. The analysis still needs the person’s coverage, the purpose of travel, the care involved, the provider and any required approval.

Original answer scaffold: name the legal/contractual route; identify whose benefit rules and price reference apply; state the authorisation and provider conditions supported by the material; then distinguish reimbursement from what the patient may still pay. Close with a specific uncertainty instead of promising a result. Choosing a route is not collecting two reimbursements for the same expense. Keep the two-part scope of the lecture intact: first understand the purchaser, contract and compensation design, then apply those distinctions to care abroad. The source’s figures and simplified court statements are not an official personal-advice tool.
