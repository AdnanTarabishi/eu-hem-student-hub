## 5-minute review

- The lecture presents two economic paths: removing barriers and harmonising national rules.
- Primary and secondary EU law are different levels, not competing synonyms.
- A regulation and a directive do not operate in the same way in national law.
- Receiving a health service across a border is part of the lecture’s freedom-of-services analysis.
- Prior authorisation, waiting times and reimbursement require separate questions.
- Article 56 case law, Regulation 883/2004 and Directive 2011/24 are distinct legal bases.
- Do not treat the EHIC as permission to travel for planned treatment.

## Two paths from integration to health policy

The lecture starts with economic integration and then adds the political, human-rights and values dimensions. In the market perspective, one path challenges national barriers to free movement. Another creates common standards through harmonisation. They can both influence health care, but they do different work: removing an obstacle is not the same as designing a shared rule.

Before analysing a problem, identify what moves: goods, a worker, a self-employed provider, the recipient of a service, or the service itself. The lecture’s active/passive services distinction is useful: patient mobility is the recipient crossing the border. This does not turn every health-policy dispute into a cross-border market case.

## Read the instrument before applying the rule

Treaties and the Charter form the primary-law layer in the slides. Regulations, directives and decisions sit in the secondary-law layer. The lecture contrasts a regulation’s direct applicability with a directive’s implementation through national law. Its diagram also flags the special question of an unimplemented directive and rights against a Member State; do not assume every directive works identically in every dispute.

Competence is a separate question. The slides distinguish exclusive, shared, and supporting competences. Their point is not that health is untouched by the EU, but that the legal basis and type of intervention must be identified. The EEA discussion similarly asks the reader to examine relevance and incorporation rather than assume that every EU measure applies everywhere in Europe.

## Watts: why the waiting list matters

In the lecture’s Watts example, a patient with hip arthritis and continuing pain seeks treatment outside her home system. The legal discussion connects an individual’s clinical circumstances to the system’s authorisation and planning rules. A waiting list is not simply a calendar number: the lecture emphasises medical assessment rather than an exclusively administrative view of delay.

A useful study sequence is: identify the treatment and home entitlement; identify the legal route; examine whether authorisation is required; examine the reasons for refusal or delay; then consider the reimbursement rule and review procedure. This is an original study scaffold based on the lecture, not a complete eligibility test.

## Three legal bases; do not collapse their conditions

The course identifies Treaty freedom-of-services case law, social-security coordination under Regulation 883/2004, and the cross-border healthcare directive. The directive also discusses information, national contact points, prescriptions and cooperation. The slides later distinguish developments in health technology assessment and health data from the original directive’s structure.

Supplementary clarification: the public-authority references distinguish necessary care during a temporary stay using the EHIC from authorised planned care using the S2 route. The directive route commonly involves paying first and applying for reimbursement under the home system’s conditions. Local cost-sharing, public-system affiliation, authorisation and eligibility still matter. The lecture’s compact route chart should not be read as “no one ever pays upfront” or “EHIC covers planned treatment”.

## Competence is not the same question as a patient’s right

*Expanded reading*

The lecture asks two different questions: which level may adopt legislation, and what rights a person can invoke in a cross-border situation. Its competence chart distinguishes exclusive EU fields, shared fields, and support, coordination or supplementation. Health appears in more than one part of that chart: protection and improvement of human health is distinguished from common safety concerns in public health. Internal-market powers are also relevant. A useful answer therefore identifies the particular activity rather than treating every health-related measure as belonging to one undifferentiated competence.

The two economic paths then explain different mechanisms of change. Challenging a national barrier can remove an obstacle in a particular legal setting; harmonisation actively establishes common standards through legislation. Neither mechanism, as presented here, means that all national health systems have been replaced. Study task: explain which mechanism is being used before discussing its consequences for patients, providers or national planning. This is a reading framework, not an independent determination that a proposed policy has a valid legal basis.

## Turn the case-law chart into a structured analysis

*Expanded reading*

The patient-mobility chart brings several questions together. Start with the treatment that the home system covers, then identify any relevant referral or other general conditions. Next separate the possibility of requiring prior authorisation from the reasons for granting or refusing it. The lecture associates hospital care and major medical equipment with planning concerns, while its non-hospital example is treated differently. That contrast should be read within the chart’s case-law setting rather than turned into an unconditional promise about every contemporary treatment abroad.

For waiting lists, the Watts discussion directs attention to a medical assessment of the patient’s circumstances, not merely an administrative place in the queue. The chart also connects authorisation procedures with accessibility, objectivity and review, and distinguishes treatment costs from travel and accommodation. A strong short answer keeps these questions separate: coverage, procedure, clinically acceptable delay, and the relevant reimbursement limit. The examples do not supply an individual patient’s clinical record, national rules or insurer decision, so those facts must remain unresolved rather than invented.

## What the Directive adds beyond paying a bill

*Expanded reading*

The lecture’s outline of Directive 2011/24/EU contains more than reimbursement provisions. It separates responsibilities of the Member State of treatment from those of the Member State of affiliation, identifies national contact points, and presents cooperation concerning prescriptions, European reference networks, rare diseases, eHealth and health technology assessment. This organisation is useful: patient mobility requires usable information and coordination as well as a route for paying for treatment. A reimbursement answer alone would miss those institutional dimensions of the lecture.

The later slide on selected topics also shows why the date and version of an instrument matter. It annotates the original outline with later instruments concerning health technology assessment, health data and reporting. Treat the earlier outline as a guide to the development of the framework, not a guarantee that every provision remains unchanged. The EEA discussion is a further distinct layer: the course introduces EEA relevance and EFTA case law, but it does not make EU membership, EEA participation and every individual entitlement interchangeable.
