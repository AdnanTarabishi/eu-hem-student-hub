## Start with the division of responsibilities


Hagen and Feiring describe municipal responsibility for primary, social and long-term care, and state administration of specialist care through regional health authorities. Their focus is efficient and fair resource use rather than an exhaustive guide to all services.

The municipal level allocates resources across several public functions and between forms of care. The specialist system uses predominantly budget-based financing with additional economic incentives. The coexistence of these mechanisms is central to the chapter’s account.


Sources: [Hagen & Feiring — Health Policy Reforms for Three Decades: Then Returning to Square One? (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780198888581.013.0031) — Oxford Handbook of Norwegian Politics, ch. 31; supplied PDF pp. 2–10


## What the reforms attempted


The chapter discusses activity-based hospital financing, the GP list system, hospital choice, state takeover of hospitals and coordination between levels. It reports improvements in activity or access alongside problems such as deficits, workload, fragmented responsibilities and concern about early discharge.

Read outcomes measure by measure. For example, the coordination package includes more than one incentive, and the authors report that not all components produced the intended effects. It would be misleading to label the entire package simply a success or a failure.

## Priority setting is both technical and normative


The chapter identifies expected benefit, severity and cost-effectiveness as central criteria. It distinguishes clinical-level judgment from group-level health technology assessment, including the use of quality-adjusted life years and absolute shortfall in the latter context.

A cost-effectiveness calculation does not remove the need to justify which outcomes matter, who bears the opportunity cost and how severity enters the decision. The chapter describes institutional procedures for managing these decisions, rather than offering a universal funding threshold.

## Who is accountable for a difficult decision?


Delegation to expert bodies can create consistency and protect decisions from short-term pressures. The authors also discuss the risk that politically and ethically contested questions become treated as merely technical, reducing public or elected influence.

Their concluding concern is that scaling back incentives while demand and workforce pressures remain might revive waiting-time and priority-setting difficulties. This is the authors’ argument about a risk, not proof that the system has already returned to an earlier state. An original seminar question is how to preserve expertise while keeping decisions transparent and contestable.
