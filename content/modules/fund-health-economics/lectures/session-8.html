01 / The bridge from indemnity

Insurance stops being a simple contingent claim when “the right treatment” is not known in advance.

A fixed indemnity works cleanly when one event implies one known loss. Healthcare insurance must instead finance an uncertain package of clinically appropriate care after the patient becomes ill.

SIMPLE BENCHMARK

One disease

The contingency is easy to define and price.

SIMPLE BENCHMARK

One treatment

The expenditure required in the sick state is predictable.

MODERN CARE

Many feasible paths

Diagnosis, severity, comorbidity and preferences change what care is appropriate.

Fixed contingencyPredetermined indemnity
→
Clinical complexityUncertain appropriate treatment
→
Insurance flexibilityActual use may diverge from appropriate care
Core definition for this session: moral hazard is not simply “bad character.” It is a change in behaviour or utilisation caused by insurance protection, so expected loss becomes larger than it would have been under undistorted incentives.

02 / Expected loss

Insurance can act on the probability of illness, the cost after illness, or both.

E[L] = p(eP) × L(eL)
PREVENTION MARGIN

eP changes p

Vaccination, monitoring and preventive behaviour affect how likely the insured event is.

LOSS-REDUCTION MARGIN

eL changes L

Treatment intensity, substitution toward lower-cost alternatives and recovery effort affect the size of the realised loss.

03 / Timing

Ex-ante and ex-post moral hazard alter different parts of expected loss.

BEFORE THE EVENT

Ex-ante moral hazard

Insurance weakens prevention and raises the probability of the event. Think of skipped preventive checks or vaccination.

eP ↓ ⇒ p(eP) ↑
AFTER THE EVENT

Ex-post moral hazard

Once sick, the insured uses a more costly recovery path or consumes care with positive private benefit but cost above that benefit.

eL ↓ ⇒ L(eL) ↑
Exam distinction: adverse selection changes who buys which contract before risk is realised; moral hazard changes behaviour or care use because coverage changes incentives.

04 / Standard FFS insurance

Three design features reinforce one another.

01Providers paid fee for serviceMore services generate more revenue.
02Low patient price at useInsurance reduces out-of-pocket cost per unit.
03Broad provider choicePatients can select providers with limited plan control.

The patient and provider may jointly favour additional care: the patient receives another service at a subsidised price, while the FFS provider receives additional payment. Once utilisation responds to coverage, the benchmark result that fair full insurance is first best no longer generally holds.

05 / The demand diagram

Full insurance can push consumption beyond the quantity whose marginal benefit covers marginal cost.

Original Student Hub illustration. The triangle represents services for which social marginal cost exceeds the consumer's marginal benefit.

Without insurance

The patient pays the marginal cost and stops at Q*, where marginal benefit equals marginal cost.

With full insurance

The private marginal price falls toward zero. The patient continues while marginal benefit is positive, even when it is below the resource cost.

Two determinants of moral hazard: the size of the price distortion created by insurance and the price responsiveness (elasticity) of demand.

06 / Time inconsistency and mimicking

The coverage people want before illness can differ from the care they choose after illness.

EX ANTEBuy broad coverage at a premium based on expected appropriate care.
→
HEALTH SHOCKSeverity and treatment need are imperfectly observed by the insurer.
→
EX POSTChoose any covered care with positive private marginal benefit.

If a mildly ill person can consume the high-intensity treatment intended for a more severe state, expected claims rise. The actuarially fair premium calculated after this behavioural response is therefore higher than the premium based only on first-best treatment levels.

07 / Coinsurance and deadweight loss

A patient share moves the private price closer to the social marginal cost.

k = 1

No insurance

The patient pays the whole bill. Risk protection is absent, but the price signal is undistorted.

0 < k < 1

Partial coverage

The patient pays fraction k and the insurer pays 1−k. Use is higher than under full price but lower than under full coverage.

k = 0

Full coverage

The point-of-use price is zero, maximising risk protection and the incentive distortion.

Patient price = kP   ·   Insurer share = (1−k)P

Increasing k makes insurance less generous. Utilisation and insurer expenditure fall, and the deadweight-loss triangle generally shrinks. But the patient bears more financial risk.

08 / Second best

The optimal contract balances two costs that move in opposite directions.

More generous coverage↓ Cost of risk bearingOut-of-pocket expenditure is more predictable.
⇄
More generous coverage↑ Cost of moral hazardSubsidised use increases and resources can be misallocated.
Second-best result: when moral hazard is material, optimal coverage is generally less than full. “Less than full” does not mean “no insurance”; it means accepting some patient exposure to preserve incentives.

09 / Demand-side commitment devices

Enrollees can prefer restrictions before they know whether they will need care.

%

Coinsurance

The patient pays a fraction of every covered bill.

€

Copayment

The patient pays a fixed amount for each unit or episode.

D

Deductible

The patient pays initial expenditure before coverage begins.

An alternative is a direct quantity restriction. Both approaches reduce moral hazard at a cost: greater financial exposure under cost sharing, or a greater risk of not receiving optimal care under quantity constraints.

10 / Price distortion

The same clinical service can generate very different welfare loss under different copayments.

SMALL PRICE GAP

Patient price close to marginal cost

Little extra utilisation and a small deadweight-loss area, but weaker risk protection.

LARGE PRICE GAP

Patient price far below marginal cost

More extra utilisation and a larger potential welfare loss, but stronger financial protection.

11 / Elasticity

Price-responsive demand makes a given price subsidy more distortionary.

Inelastic service

Quantity changes little when the patient price falls. The utilisation distortion and DWL are relatively small, so more complete coverage is easier to justify.

Elastic service

Quantity changes strongly when the price falls. The utilisation distortion is larger, so the optimal patient share is generally higher.

More elastic demand ⇒ higher optimal coinsurance c*
More inelastic demand ⇒ more complete optimal coverage
Do not overgeneralise: observed elasticity may reflect poor information rather than low clinical value. This is why the lecture later challenges uniform cost-sharing rules.

12 / Interrelated demand

Using more of one service can reduce—or increase—the use of another covered service.

SUBSTITUTES

Offset effect

If service A replaces an overused service B, generous coverage of A can create an efficiency gain through lower use of B.

c**sub < c*
COMPLEMENTS

Reinforcement effect

If A triggers additional use of B, generous coverage of A can magnify total expenditure and distortion.

c**comp > c*
c**sub < c* < c**comp

The same service can be a substitute in one health state and a complement in another. Physical therapy can replace pain medication in some chronic conditions but complement surgery during rehabilitation. A simple service-level copayment cannot fully encode this context.

13 / Limits of the welfare-loss argument

The demand curve is an imperfect measure of patient value in healthcare.

Information

Patients may not know the true expected benefit of a test, drug or prevention programme.

Agency

Patients may delegate treatment decisions to providers, so observed demand is partly supplier shaped.

Behaviour

Fear, anxiety and present bias can separate willingness to pay from clinical value.

The traditional DWL calculation assumes that the observed demand curve reveals marginal utility. That assumption becomes fragile when patients are poorly informed and providers influence utilisation.

14 / Preventive care and VBID

High elasticity does not automatically mean low value.

Observed factPreventive care can be highly price responsive.
≠
Incorrect shortcutTherefore prevention should face high cost sharing.
→
Value-based designLower or subsidise cost sharing for high-value care.

Value-Based Insurance Design (VBID) sets patient prices according to expected clinical value rather than applying the same cost-sharing rule to every service. In some cases, the welfare-improving patient price can be zero or even negative through a subsidy.

15 / Where is the waste?

Demand-side and provider-side theories imply different policy targets.

TRADITIONAL VIEW

Patients demand too much

Comprehensive coverage makes users insensitive to price.

Policy: increase patient cost sharing.

ALTERNATIVE VIEW

Providers deliver low-value care

The problem is not simply patient volume but production of services with limited health value.

Policy: redesign payment, review utilisation and reward value.

16 / Supply-side cost sharing

Managed care shifts part of the constraint from the patient to the delivery system.

Managed-care plans integrate insurance, provider networks and payment mechanisms. A typical managed plan limits provider choice and places some financial or organisational responsibility for resource use on providers.

Network restriction

Coverage terms steer patients toward contracted providers.

Provider financial risk

Payment is less purely volume based and can expose providers to the cost of additional care.

Utilisation management

The plan reviews appropriateness or requires authorisation before selected services.

Different risk: demand-side cost sharing exposes patients mainly to financial risk; supply-side restrictions expose them mainly to the risk that useful care is delayed or denied because it is judged not cost-effective.

17 / Heterogeneous need

One copayment or one quantity cap cannot perfectly treat moderately and seriously ill patients.

M
Moderately illLower marginal benefit and lower first-best quantity.
S
Seriously illHigher marginal benefit and higher first-best quantity.

If the insurer cannot distinguish the two types, a common copayment reduces use for both. A common quantity limit can deliver the correct total amount on average while giving too much to M and too little to S. Neither a pure demand-side rule nor a pure supply-side rule guarantees the first best for every patient.

Combination logic: a small copayment can deter low-value use by M before it deters high-value use by S. That screening effect can allow a higher quantity limit for patients who continue to demand care. Price and quantity instruments can therefore outperform either instrument alone.

18 / How plans control cost and quality

Managed care uses several mechanisms, not one single payment rule.

  1. 01

  2. 02
    Quality assurance

    Use accreditation and performance standards.

  3. 03
    Utilisation review

    Assess whether provider practices and requested services are appropriate.

  4. 04
    Gatekeeping and second opinions

    Require referral or additional review before selected specialist care.

20 / Managed plans as commitment devices

Choosing a plan means choosing coverage, a provider network and provider incentives together.

At enrolmentConsumer anticipates future time inconsistency
→
Plan choiceSelects network and payment rules in advance
→
When sickThose rules constrain low-value additional use

A consumer may rationally prefer an MCO ex ante even though, once sick, the same consumer would prefer unrestricted access. Precommitment can raise expected utility by lowering the premium and moderating moral hazard.

21 / Empirical evidence in the lecture

Managed care can lower expenditure without necessarily lowering measured utilisation.

CUTLER & SHEINER

Spending growth

The lecture reports that a 10-percentage-point increase in HMO enrolment was associated with 0.5% lower growth in hospital spending.

GLIED REVIEW

Expenditure

The review reported no clear lower utilisation rate, while MCO expenditure was 10–15% lower.

AMI / IHD STUDY

Prices and quality

Similar treatment patterns and little measured quality difference accompanied lower costs, mainly through lower negotiated prices per service.

Interpretation: lower expenditure can come from lower negotiated prices, lower quantities through review, lower quality, or slower adoption of technology. Expenditure alone does not identify which mechanism is operating.

22 / Innovation and long-run welfare

Cost containment today can affect technology and quality tomorrow.

Potential gain

Slower adoption can prevent diffusion of expensive technologies with limited value and reduce wasteful price competition.

Potential loss

Lower profitability and weaker patient-centred competition can delay valuable innovation and forego future health improvements.

The concluding policy problem is therefore multidimensional: reduce moral hazard and reallocate surplus without sacrificing appropriate care, quality or socially valuable innovation.

EXAM SYNTHESIS

Use this reasoning sequence in MCQs, binary traps and open policy questions.

1Identify the information and incentive problem.Ex-ante MH, ex-post MH, provider incentive or selection?
2Locate the distorted margin.Probability p, loss L, patient price, service quantity or provider choice?
3Name the instrument.Copay, coinsurance, deductible, quantity cap, payment reform or managed-care control?
4State the intended mechanism.How does the instrument change behaviour, utilisation, price or plan design?
5Discuss the trade-off.Risk protection, access, equity, quality, innovation and administrative burden.
6Recommend a targeted mix.Avoid treating all services and all patients as identical.
Common exam trap: “more utilisation with insurance” is evidence consistent with moral hazard, but it is not by itself proof of waste. Additional insured care may have high value, may substitute for other expenditure, or may reflect provider influence and unmet need.

5-MINUTE REVIEW

The ten distinctions to retain.

Healthcare insurance covers uncertain appropriate treatment, not merely a fixed event loss.
Ex-ante moral hazard changes event probability; ex-post moral hazard changes loss after the event.
Full coverage can drive the private marginal price below social marginal cost and create overuse.
The extent of moral hazard depends on price distortion and demand elasticity.
More elastic demand generally calls for higher optimal coinsurance; inelastic services can be covered more fully.
Cost sharing preserves incentives but weakens risk protection and may reduce high-value care.
Substitution, complementarity, imperfect information and physician agency limit simple service-level rules.
VBID lowers patient prices for high-value care rather than treating all utilisation as equally wasteful.
Managed care combines networks, provider incentives, utilisation review and gatekeeping.
Lower MCO expenditure may reflect lower prices, quantities, quality or technology diffusion; welfare requires more than a spending comparison.
