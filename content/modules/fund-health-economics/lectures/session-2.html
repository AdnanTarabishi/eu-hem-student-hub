01 / Start with the curve

Price changes quantity. Other changes move demand.

Session 2 begins with the distinction that drives almost everything else: movement along a demand curve versus a shift of the curve.

Do not mix these up. The lecture later shows how observational data can make a shift caused by health risk look like a slope effect caused by price.

02 / Value on the demand curve

Willingness to pay turns demand into value.

The demand curve can be read as buyers ordered by how much they are willing to pay.

WTP

Willingness to pay

The maximum amount a buyer would pay for a unit. It measures how much that unit is valued.

CS

Consumer surplus

WTP minus the price actually paid. The gap is the buyer's surplus from participating in the market.

0

Marginal consumer

The active buyer with the lowest WTP. At the market price, this buyer receives zero consumer surplus.

Consumer surplus = willingness to pay − price paid

03 / Why this matters in healthcare

Demand is not an abstract graph.

The course gives three reasons to estimate healthcare demand: insurance design, equity and long-term capacity planning.

Insurance

Cost-sharing changes the price patients face. Insurers need to know how utilization responds.

Equity

Differences in access and use across groups raise questions about who receives care and who bears its cost.

Planning

Hospitals, medical schools and workforce decisions require forecasts of future healthcare use.

05 / Randomization

RAND and Oregon create cleaner comparisons.

Both are used in the lecture to study healthcare use under different prices or insurance conditions.

RAND HEALTH INSURANCE EXPERIMENT

Direct randomization of insurance generosity.

The lecture describes families randomly assigned to plans with different coinsurance rates and then compares healthcare utilization and outcomes.

Course takeaway: higher patient cost-sharing reduced use, supporting downward-sloping healthcare demand.
OREGON MEDICAID EXPERIMENT

Randomized opportunity to apply for Medicaid.

A lottery among low-income adults created random variation in access to public insurance and therefore in expected out-of-pocket prices.

Course takeaway: winners used more outpatient care, while results varied across other types of care.

06 / Not all care responds equally

Urgency and necessity shape price sensitivity.

OP

Outpatient

The lecture presents clear evidence of a downward-sloping demand response.

IP

Inpatient

Still price-responsive in RAND, but less elastic than outpatient care in the lecture's comparison.

ER

Emergency

Urgent care can be less price-sensitive, but the lecture stresses that response need not be zero.

Clinical value is not the same as price sensitivity. Session 2 highlights evidence that higher out-of-pocket prices can reduce use without perfectly separating effective care from low-value care.

07 / Elasticity

How much does quantity respond?

ε = % change in quantity / % change in price

|ε| < 1

Inelastic. Quantity changes proportionally less than price.

|ε| = 1

Unit elastic. The percentage changes are equal in magnitude.

|ε| > 1

Elastic. Quantity changes proportionally more than price.

Arc elasticity: use midpoint changes between two points

εarc = [(Q₂ − Q₁) / ((Q₁ + Q₂)/2)] ÷ [(P₂ − P₁) / ((P₁ + P₂)/2)]

The midpoint approach makes the comparison symmetric and unit-free. Use the calculator in Explore.

09 / Price and health are different outcomes

Using less care does not automatically mean worse measured health.

RAND

The lecture reports clear utilization differences by cost-sharing but limited overall differences in objective health outcomes, with some effects among particular high-risk groups and selected measures.

Oregon

The lecture reports better self-reported health, more healthy days and lower depression among lottery winners, while emphasizing that self-reported health differs from objective health and follow-up was shorter.

10 / Insurance changes the patient price

Four contract forms introduced in Session 2.

FIXED INDEMNITY

A fixed insurer contribution.

The insurer pays a fixed amount for the service and the patient remains responsible for the residual price.

COINSURANCE

A percentage of the bill.

The patient pays a specified fraction and the insurer pays the rest.

COPAYMENT

A fixed amount per use.

The patient pays the same specified amount for each covered unit of care.

DEDUCTIBLE

Pay first, then coverage starts.

The patient must meet an initial amount of spending before reimbursement begins.

12 / Two-minute review

The logic of Session 2.

Demand curve

Own price → movement along the curve. Other determinants → shift.

Value

WTP measures value; consumer surplus = WTP − price.

Evidence

Observational data can be confounded by insurance selection; RAND and Oregon provide randomized variation.

Elasticity

Healthcare demand is downward sloping and generally inelastic, not perfectly inelastic.

Full price

Money + travel + opportunity cost of time.

Insurance

Indemnity, coinsurance, copayment and deductible change the effective price faced by the patient.
