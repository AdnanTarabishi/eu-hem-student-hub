01 / Start with the problem

Medical expenditure is uncertain, concentrated and potentially catastrophic.

Session 6 explains why people pay a predictable premium today to protect income against a large uncertain loss tomorrow.

People do not know with certainty whether the covered illness will occur.

M

N

Pooling

A large group shares expected expenditure so that no single enrollee must self-finance the entire possible loss.

What insurance covers in this lecture: the financial risk associated with medical care. The contract does not remove illness itself.

03 / Three responses to a health shock

Borrow, save or insure.

BORROW

Pay after illness occurs

Repayment can be difficult precisely when the person is debilitated or financially weakened.

SAVE

Self-insure in advance

Everyone must restrict current consumption enough to prepare for a rare cost borne by only some people.

POOL

Share expected expenditure

Each member pays a premium and the pool finances indemnities for those who become sick.

04 / Risk aversion

The same expected income can produce different utility.

Risk aversion is represented by an increasing, concave utility function.

U′(y) > 0

More income gives more utility.

The utility curve slopes upward.

U″(y) < 0

Marginal utility declines.

A one-euro loss at low income hurts more than a one-euro gain at high income helps.

THE KEY INEQUALITY

Utility of expected income exceeds expected utility under risk.

U(E[Y]) > E[U(Y)]

For a non-degenerate risk and a strictly concave utility function, certainty is preferred to a risky prospect with the same expected income.

Fair gamble versus fair insurance: a fair gamble adds risk to certainty; fair insurance removes risk from an already uncertain income. A risk-averse person can reject the first and prefer the second.

05 / Expected value and expected utility

Keep the monetary calculation separate from the utility calculation.

EXPECTED INCOME
E[Y] = Σ pᵢyᵢ

A probability-weighted average of monetary outcomes.

EXPECTED UTILITY
E[U(Y)] = Σ pᵢU(yᵢ)

A probability-weighted average of the utility generated by each outcome.

06 / Certainty equivalent

Translate a risky prospect into a certain-income equivalent.

Risky incomeE[U(Y)]
=
Same utility under certaintyU(y*)
→
Risk premiumE[Y] − y*

The risk premium is a utility-based willingness-to-pay measure. It is not the same thing as the insurance premium charged by a contract.

07 / The benchmark contract

Five symbols organise the whole session.

yIncome before medical spending
pProbability of sickness
MMedical loss or treatment cost
IIndemnity paid if sick
πPremium paid in advance

Income after buying insurance

HEALTHY
yh = y − π

The premium is paid, but no indemnity is received.

SICK
ys = y − π − M + I

The medical loss occurs and the insurer pays the indemnity.

Benchmark assumptions: one covered disease, one treatment, a fixed medical loss and no post-contract behavioural response. These assumptions isolate the demand for insurance.

A large pool collects premiums from all enrollees and pays indemnities to the expected fraction who become sick. Under the benchmark, the fair premium per person equals expected indemnity expenditure.

09 / Demand for fair insurance

As coverage rises, healthy income falls and sick income rises.

Under fair pricing, π = pI. Therefore:

yh = y − pI

One more euro of indemnity costs p euros in the healthy state.

ys = y − M + (1 − p)I

One more euro of indemnity raises net sick-state income by 1 − p.

10 / The optimum

A fair contract leads the risk-averse consumer to full insurance.

Choose I to maximisepU(ys) + (1−p)U(yh)
First-order conditionU′(ys) = U′(yh)
Strict concavity impliesys = yh
Substitute state incomesI* = M
Full insurance is the benchmark optimum because it equalises income and marginal utility across the healthy and sick states. This conclusion is conditional on fair pricing and the model assumptions.

11 / Full insurance

State independence is the defining result.

FULL INSURANCE
I = M

Income is equal across states: ys = yh.

PARTIAL INSURANCE
I < M

Some loss remains uninsured: ys < yh.

12 / The insurer

Look at the same contract from the supply side.

E[Π] = (1−p)π + p(π−I) = π − pI

π = pI

Zero expected profit: actuarially fair in the competitive benchmark.

π > pI

Positive expected profit: called unfair in the lecture's technical terminology.

π < pI

Negative expected profit: not sustainable for a profit-seeking insurer in this benchmark.

14 / Ranking contracts

The ideal contract is clear; non-ideal rankings are not.

IDEAL BENCHMARK

Fair and full

For a risk-averse person in this model, it offers the greatest utility because it removes the risk at expected cost.

NON-IDEAL COMPARISON

Full but loaded versus fair but partial

The ranking depends on both premium and coverage, together with the individual's risk aversion. Labels alone do not determine the winner.

17 / Two-minute review

The logic of Session 6.

Why insurance?

Medical spending is uncertain and may produce a large income loss. Pooling converts that risk into a predictable premium.

Why does risk aversion matter?

Concave utility makes a low-income loss more painful than an equal high-income gain is valuable.

What is the fair premium?

π = pI: the premium equals expected indemnity expenditure.

Why full insurance?

At a fair premium, optimal coverage equalises income and marginal utility across states, giving I* = M.

What is insurer profit?

E[Π] = π − pI.

What distinction must not be confused?

Full versus partial depends on indemnity size; fair versus unfair depends on premium size.
