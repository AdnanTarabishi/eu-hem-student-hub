01 / The policy question

Why have social insurance instead of relying only on voluntary private contracts?

Session 7 starts from the risk-reduction value of insurance, then asks why hidden risk types can prevent a competitive market from delivering the contracts people would want under complete information.

R

Risk reduction

Insurance smooths consumption across illness, disability, unemployment and other adverse states.

I

Incentive distortion

Policies can change behaviour by workers, firms and insurers, creating deadweight loss.

2

Second best

Correcting one market failure can create new distortions, so the target becomes the best feasible policy rather than a frictionless first best.

Lecture framing: social insurance transfers resources across states or time linked to events such as illness, disability, unemployment and ageing. Means-tested welfare is presented as redistribution across individuals.

02 / Plan competition

Competition can improve efficiency, but the identity of the buyer changes expected cost.

Consumers searching for lower premiums can pressure plans to control costs and innovate. Health insurance is unusual because two people buying the same benefits may generate very different expected claims.

03 / Two kinds of variation

Random variation creates insurance demand; systematic variation shapes who can afford it.

RANDOM / RESIDUAL

Why insurance exists

Individuals do not know whether a costly event will occur. Pooling converts uncertain individual losses into more predictable group expenditure.

SYSTEMATIC / PREDICTABLE

Why insurance markets become difficult

Age, health status and other predictors create persistent expected-cost differences. These differences affect premiums, selection and equity.

Thought experiment: if variation were fully random, uniform premiums could work well. If each person's spending were perfectly predictable, insurance would no longer create utility gains from risk reduction; the issue would become prepayment and redistribution.

04 / Asymmetric information

Adverse selection begins when the consumer knows more about expected losses.

Low riskpL
High riskpH > pL
Low-risk shareλ
Mixed-pool probabilityp̄ = λpL + (1−λ)pH

If risk were observable and type-specific pricing were used, fair premiums would be πL=pLI and πH=pHI. When plans charge an average premium, the low risk pays more than own expected cost and the high risk pays less.

πP = [λpL + (1−λ)pH]I

More generous plans can therefore become expensive for two reasons: they pay a larger indemnity and they attract a sicker mix of enrollees. The second mechanism is adverse selection.

05 / Reframing the basic insurance model

Represent a contract as income in the healthy and sick states.

ENDOWMENT
E=(y, y−M)

Before insurance, healthy-state income is y and sick-state income is y−M.

CONTRACT (π,I)
C=(y−π, y−π−M+I)

The premium moves the point left; the net payout I−π moves it upward.

06 / Preferences in contract space

Indifference curves are downward sloping, convex and type dependent.

Downward sloping

More income in one state can compensate for less income in the other.

Convex

Risk aversion makes balanced income across states valuable.

Single crossing

The high-risk/frail type places more relative weight on sick-state income, producing a flatter indifference curve.

Economic meaning: for the same net indemnity, the frail type is willing to give up more healthy-state income because the sick state occurs more often.

07 / Zero-profit lines

Each risk probability generates a different break-even frontier.

p(I−π) = (1−p)π

Using the contract coordinates, the zero-profit line through the endowment is:

yS = y−M + [(1−p)/p](y−yH)

Its slope is −(1−p)/p. A higher p produces a flatter line. The pooling zero-profit line lies between the low-risk and high-risk lines.

BELOW THE LINE

Expected profit

The contract charges enough relative to coverage for that risk type.

ON THE LINE

Break even

The premium equals expected payout.

ABOVE THE LINE

Expected loss

A competitive insurer will not voluntarily offer the contract.

08 / The feasible contract wedge

Consumers and insurers must both be willing to participate.

Exclude overinsurance

Points above the 45-degree line give more income when sick than when healthy.

Respect participation

A consumer rejects contracts below the indifference curve through the no-insurance endowment.

Respect profitability

Firms reject contracts that lie in the expected-loss region.

09 / Rothschild–Stiglitz environment

Two hidden risk types choose among contracts offered by competitive insurers.

PEOPLE

Know their own type

Low-risk L and high-risk H share the same income and loss but differ in p.

INSURERS

Know only the population mix

They observe λ but cannot identify each person's type.

MARKET

Competitive and static

No moral hazard, zero-profit firms and one contract purchased per individual.

Equilibrium requires two things: consumers cannot find a preferred offered contract, and firms earn zero expected profit.

10 / Why first best fails

Type-specific full insurance cannot survive when the insurer cannot observe type.

+

Hidden informationH imitates the cheaper L contract

Once high risks enter the low-risk contract, premium revenue no longer covers expected claims. The first-best menu is therefore not incentive compatible.

12 / Why pooling is not a market equilibrium

Competition can cream-skim the profitable low-risk group.

StartBoth types choose Z
Low-risk problemZ is less than fair for L
EntrantOffer Q preferred by L, rejected by H
ResultQ earns profit and Z unravels

The single-crossing property makes it possible to design a contract that attracts the robust type without attracting the frail type. The pooling contract therefore cannot survive free entry in the standard argument.

13 / Separating equilibrium

The market may sort types, but low risks pay for separation with incomplete coverage.

HIGH RISK H

Fair full insurance

The high-risk contract CH lies on the H zero-profit line and reaches the certainty line.

LOW RISK L

Fair partial insurance

The low-risk contract C′L lies on the L zero-profit line but is restricted so H does not prefer it.

Quantity rationing: L cannot obtain its preferred full insurance even at its own fair premium. The missing coverage is the informational cost of making the menu incentive compatible.

14 / Equilibrium may not exist

The population composition λ changes whether separation is stable.

When low risks are not too numerous, the separating pair can survive. When λ is large, an alternative pooling contract can attract enough low risks to be profitable and preferred, destabilising the separating menu. The lecture therefore allows a case in which the competitive market reaches no equilibrium.

λ relatively small

A separating equilibrium may exist.

λ rises

The welfare loss from low-risk rationing affects more people.

λ sufficiently large

A profitable deviation may destroy separation; no market equilibrium may remain.

15 / Government intervention

A compulsory pooling base can be efficiency improving, not only redistributive.

The government can impose a basic pooling contract G that breaks even for the population:

πG = [λpL+(1−λ)pH]IG

The mandate moves the common endowment, transfers from L to H and can reduce the coverage distortion required for later separation.

Layer 1Compulsory basic pooling
+
Layer 2Supplementary private contracts
→
Possible resultH receives subsidy; L approaches fuller coverage

Equivalent partial-coverage policy

Tax partial insurance and subsidise fuller insurance to alter the separation cost.

Route to full coverage

Mandated pooling contracts or compulsory public insurance can prevent selection from excluding the frail.

Important qualification: the model does not claim that every mandate improves welfare. The compulsory contract must be chosen so the new allocation is feasible and preferred.

16 / Premium risk

Insurance today does not automatically insure the risk of becoming expensive tomorrow.

As information accumulates over the life course, expected remaining healthcare expenditure diverges across people. A competitive spot market tends to align each contract's premium with expected cost, exposing newly frail people to higher future premiums.

LIFETIME CONTRACT

Bind the pool for life

Solves repricing through commitment, but may be difficult to enforce and blocks switching.

GUARANTEED RENEWABLE

Front-load premiums

Keep both robust and frail consumers voluntarily, but insurer mobility and competitive pressure remain limited.

PREMIUM INSURANCE

Compensate health-status shocks

A deterioration triggers a payment that helps finance higher future premiums, as in the Cochrane-style idea presented in the lecture.
