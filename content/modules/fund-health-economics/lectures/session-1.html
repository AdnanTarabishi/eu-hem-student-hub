01 / Find your bearings

What is health economics really studying?

Not simply the price of care. It studies choices, scarce resources and incentives across the whole health system.

1

People

How patients value health, seek care, respond to prices and make choices under uncertainty.

2

Providers

How physicians and organisations respond to information, professional goals and payment incentives.

3

Institutions

How insurers and governments finance care, pool risk, regulate markets and shape access.

The central constraint is scarcity. Health can be extremely valuable while the staff, time, facilities, technology and money used to produce it remain limited.

03 / Spending growth

Why does healthcare expenditure expand?

The lecture groups the main drivers into economic, demographic, technological and institutional forces.

Income

As economies become richer, they can devote more resources to healthcare.

Technology

New diagnostics and treatments create new possibilities to spend resources in exchange for health gains.

Ageing

Population structure changes healthcare needs and the mix of services demanded.

Value of health

Societies may choose to devote more resources to extending life and improving health.

Insurance

Financial protection changes how care is financed and the price patients face at the point of use.

Institutions

Rules, political choices and resource endowments help explain why similarly rich countries still differ.

S

Sustainability

How much healthcare spending can or should a society support, and what is sacrificed when healthcare absorbs more resources?

E

Equity

Who should contribute to financing care, and how should access be distributed when needs and ability to pay differ?

05 / Value

More spending is not the same as more value.

Economists distinguish the overall contribution of healthcare from the health gain produced by the next unit of spending.

AVG

Average productivity

Looks at health outcomes relative to the overall level of healthcare resources already being used.

Δ

Marginal productivity

Asks how much extra health is produced by one additional unit of healthcare resources.

Large total benefits can coexist with small gains from the next euro spent.

08 / Public action

Why does government enter healthcare?

The session separates what government does from why it may intervene.

R

Regulator

Sets rules and changes private costs or benefits to better reflect social goals.

I

Insurer

Collects resources in advance and assumes part of the financial burden when healthcare is needed.

P

Provider

Directly produces or supplies healthcare services.

Efficiency

Can intervention reduce waste or correct a market failure so that more value is produced from available resources?

Redistribution

Even an efficient allocation can be considered unfair. Public policy may therefore change who contributes and who benefits.

10 / Compare systems

Four broad teaching models.

Use these as simplified analytical categories. Real health systems mix features and evolve over time.

BEVERIDGE

Strong public role

The lecture associates this model with single-payer financing, a large public role in provision, limited cost-sharing and strong emphasis on equity and expenditure control.

BISMARCK

Compulsory insurance

The course framework combines compulsory insurance with mainly private providers and substantial government regulation.

AMERICAN

Larger private-market role

The teaching model gives private markets a central role alongside public coverage for selected groups.

CASH-BASED

Direct payment dominates

Greater reliance on out-of-pocket payment and limited insurance makes access and affordability especially important.

Do not treat the four labels as perfect descriptions of real countries. They are broad teaching models used in the session to compare financing, provision, regulation, choice and equity.
