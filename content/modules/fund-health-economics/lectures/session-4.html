Health differs by education, income and rank.

The observation comes first. The causal story comes later.

Occupational and social hierarchy can also track health, motivating stress and status explanations.

Correlation is the starting point, not the answer. Session 4 asks whether SES causes health, health causes SES, or another factor causes both.

02 / Three arrows

What causes what?

DIRECT

SES → Health

Education, resources, stress, inequality or access may shape health.

REVERSE

Health → SES

Illness can reduce productive time, earnings and labour-force participation.

THIRD FACTOR

X → SES + Health

A common determinant such as time preference can influence both.

WHY NATURAL?

Random deprivation would be unethical.

Historical shocks can provide comparison groups and reduce some selection problems without deliberately assigning harm.

05 / Income

More resources can expand the health PPF — but evidence differs by age.

Adults

The lecture presents little consistent causal evidence that higher income alone improves adult health in older developed-country samples and describes lottery evidence as mixed.

Children

Parental income appears more protective for child health. Early health insults can accumulate and continue into adulthood.

The lecture's point is not “income never matters.” It is that the causal effect is not uniform across populations and life stages.

INTERPRETATION

Rank may matter beyond absolute income.

The lecture discusses hierarchy, control and stress as possible pathways after populations move beyond extreme poverty.

07 / Community-level mechanism

The income-inequality hypothesis.

Social cohesion

Trust and social connectedness may deteriorate as inequality rises.

Psychosocial stress

Greater hierarchy or comparison may create chronic stress.

Political pathway

The lecture discusses the possibility that extreme inequality skews politics toward the rich and powerful.

Evidence ≠ settled causality. The lecture explicitly says the causal link between inequality and health is not clearly understood.

11 / Policy depends on mechanism

The same gradient can imply different interventions.

If education raises health-production efficiency

Education, simpler self-management or more supportive care may matter.

If early-life conditions matter

Supporting families and protecting maternal and child health becomes central.

If health lowers SES

Preventing illness and reducing its employment and earnings consequences can narrow socioeconomic gaps.

12 / Final synthesis

No single theory wins every case.

Direct: education

Efficient health production, information, innovation and self-management.

Direct: early life

Thrifty phenotype and natural-experiment evidence on prenatal and childhood shocks.

Direct: income

Resource channel, with different evidence for adults and children.

Direct: stress / inequality

Allostatic load, rank and community-level inequality mechanisms.

Reverse: productive time

Health shocks can reduce income, wealth, work and schooling.

Third factor: time preference

Long-term orientation can influence both education and health investment.
