03 / Define PID precisely

Not every physician influence is induced demand.

DUE INFLUENCE

Supports the patient's interest

Example used in the lecture: influencing a patient to stop smoking.

PID / UNDUE INFLUENCE

Moves demand against the patient's interest

The physician influences demand in a direction the physician does not interpret as best for the patient.

04 / Benchmark model

Income pulls one way. Ethics can pull the other.

π

Net income

More profitable activity can increase physician utility.

L

Leisure

Physicians also value time outside additional service production.

I

Inducement disutility

Inducing care that is not in the patient's interest carries a professional or personal cost.

The benchmark model can generate a non-zero equilibrium level of inducement even though the physician dislikes inducing, because inducement also raises income.

05 / Lower profit rate

One change, two opposing effects.

S

Substitution effect

Inducement pays less at the margin, so the physician has an incentive to induce less.

Y

Income effect

Income falls, which can make extra inducement attractive as a way to restore income.

Net response = substitution effect + income effect

If the income effect dominates, the lecture's McGuire–Pauly synthesis allows a lower profit rate to produce more inducement and quantity.

06 / Ethics

Financial incentives are not the whole physician objective.

Professional ethics changes the cost of inducement. A physician who strongly dislikes manipulating patient demand will respond differently from one for whom inducement carries little disutility.

07 / Evidence

The empirical record is mixed.

Income-effect evidence

The lecture discusses examples where fee cuts or income shocks are followed by higher procedure volumes.

Substitution-effect evidence

It also discusses evidence where lower relative payment for a procedure reduces its use.

PID tests usually identify changes in inducement after a shock, not the absolute level of inducement that existed beforehand.

08 / Towards optimal fees

Payment policy has to handle several distortions at once.

Patient incentives

Demand-side moral hazard affects how patients use care.

Provider incentives

Physicians may change volume, quality or patient selection in response to payment.

Agency

Physicians may influence the demand curve itself.

The slide text states a bottom-line result of fees below marginal cost with a two-part payment scheme. The intermediate derivation is not reproduced here because the available slide text does not contain the full mathematical steps.

09 / Payment methods

Different contracts create different activity incentives.

FFS

Fee-for-service

Payment is linked to each service delivered; volume incentives are relatively high-powered.

CAPITATION

Per-patient payment

Payment is less directly linked to each additional service.

SALARY

Fixed remuneration

Service volume is less directly tied to physician income.

Session 5 also lists blended approaches such as capitation with FFS carve-outs and case rates.

10 / Welfare question

If the physician shifts demand, what does the demand curve mean?

Ordinary welfare analysis interprets the demand curve as reflecting consumers' willingness to pay. Under strong PID, observed demand partly reflects provider influence, so the curve may no longer be a clean measure of independent patient preferences.

11 / Practice variation

Where you live can change the treatment you receive.

S

Supply matters

The SAV literature in the lecture finds supply variables statistically and materially important.

D

Demand matters too

Patient characteristics contribute, but the lecture presents them as somewhat less important than supply factors.

Even after measured supply and demand factors are included, substantial geographic variation remains unexplained.

12 / The grey area

Clinical discretion leaves room for practice style.

Why discretion persists

Clinical guidance can be limited, marginal harm can be small, and benefits can depend strongly on the individual patient.

Practice-style hypothesis

Uneven diffusion of information and incomplete knowledge create persistent differences in physician beliefs and treatment choices.

13 / Final synthesis

Physicians can be imperfect agents for two different reasons.

Self-interest

Financial incentives can affect inducement, volume and treatment choice.

Information

Physicians may themselves be uncertain or incompletely informed.

Payment design

FFS, capitation, salary and blended methods shift the strength and direction of incentives.

Practice variation

Geographic variation can persist because clinical evidence leaves room for discretion and information diffuses unevenly.
