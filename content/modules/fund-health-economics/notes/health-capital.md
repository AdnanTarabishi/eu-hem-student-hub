## 5-minute review
- The Grossman model asks whether people partly choose health, not only healthcare.
- Health is a stock of capital that persists over time, produces healthy days and can be influenced but not fully controlled.
- Healthcare is a derived demand because it is one input used to produce health.
- Health has three roles in the model: capital, consumption good and input into production.
- At very low health, better health can release enough sick time that health and home production rise together: the free-lunch zone.
- In the usual single-period optimum, someone who values both H and Z chooses on the downward-sloping trade-off portion of the PPF.
- Health evolves as $H_t=(1-\delta_t)H_{t-1}+I_{t-1}$; net investment is gross investment minus depreciation.
- The MEC is high when health is low and falls as health rises; optimal health balances marginal return against the user cost $r+\delta$.
- Ageing raises depreciation and lowers optimal health; higher wages and education raise optimal health; lower medical prices raise optimal health.
- Effects on healthcare demand need not match effects on health: age and education are ambiguous in the lecture's healthcare-demand comparative statics.

## Notes

### 1. From demand for healthcare to demand for health

Session 2 showed that healthcare use responds to price and insurance. Session 3 asks a deeper question: **do people also choose health itself?**

The Grossman model treats individuals as rational and forward-looking. They use available information and consider future as well as current costs and benefits. This does not remove uncertainty.

### 2. Health as an asset

Health is a current status shaped by the past and present that provides services now and in the future. It therefore has the character of a **capital stock** rather than something that disappears at the end of each day.

Poor health also restricts what a person can do. Work, home production and even enjoyment of consumption require time and functioning. Health therefore enables other activities.

### 3. Health can be produced — and sometimes sacrificed

Individuals can influence health by combining:

- time devoted to health-producing activities;
- purchased healthcare inputs.

Healthcare is therefore a **derived demand**: it is demanded because it helps produce health.

The lecture also recognizes unhealthy behaviours: choices that trade future health for another goal or short-term benefit.

Individual control remains limited. Genetics, environment and chance also shape health.

### 4. The three roles of health

The Grossman model gives health three roles:

1. **A stock of capital** carried across periods.
2. **A consumption good** that directly contributes to utility.
3. **An input into production** because better health creates healthy or productive time.

This is why demand for health can be analyzed through both consumption and investment motives.

### 5. Single-period model: health H and home good Z

In the consumption version, utility depends on health **H** and a home good **Z**.

The home good means everything other than health that contributes to utility. Both H and Z require time and market goods to produce, and education can improve production efficiency.

The central constraint is time. The lecture decomposes total time into time used to improve health, produce other goods, work, and time lost to illness.

### 6. The Grossman production possibility frontier

A standard downward-sloping PPF would miss an important feature of health.

At very low health, almost all time may be lost to illness. A small health improvement can release a large amount of productive time. In this **free-lunch zone**, health and the home good can rise together.

Eventually diminishing returns set in. At higher health levels, another health improvement releases only a small amount of additional productive time while still consuming time and resources. The frontier therefore becomes downward sloping and health competes with home production.

A person who values both H and Z normally chooses on this trade-off portion where the highest attainable indifference curve is tangent to the PPF.

### 7. Pure investment case and productive time

The lecture then temporarily removes direct utility from health.

Health becomes a **pure investment asset**: people invest in health because it increases productive time. More productive time creates more opportunity to work, earn income, produce home goods and invest further in health.

This connects health investment to the familiar labour–leisure problem.

### 8. Health investment over time

Health capital deteriorates.

The lecture writes the stock equation as:

$$
H_t=(1-\delta_t)H_{t-1}+I_{t-1}
$$

where:

- $H_{t-1}$ is inherited health capital;
- $\delta_t$ is the depreciation or deterioration rate;
- $I_{t-1}$ is gross health investment.

Rearranging:

$$
H_t-H_{t-1}=I_{t-1}-\delta_tH_{t-1}
$$

The right-hand side is **net health investment**.

Gross investment cannot be negative in the lecture's setup, but net investment can be:

- if $I>\delta H$, health stock rises;
- if $I=\delta H$, health stock is maintained;
- if $I<\delta H$, health stock declines.

### 9. Stocks and flows

Health capital **H** is a stock.

Gross investment **I** is a flow into that stock. Healthy days are also a flow of services generated by the stock.

Keeping stocks and flows separate is essential for understanding the multi-period model.

### 10. The marginal efficiency of health capital

In the pure-investment model, one extra unit of health capital can create additional productive time over the person's remaining life.

The **marginal efficiency of health capital (MEC)** summarizes the return to another unit of health capital.

The lecture assumes diminishing returns: when health is low, a small improvement can create a large gain in productive time; when health is already high, additional gains are smaller. The MEC therefore falls as health stock rises.

### 11. The user cost of health capital

Health investment has an opportunity cost.

Resources used for health could earn a return elsewhere, represented by **r**, and health capital also depreciates at rate **δ**.

The lecture therefore compares the MEC with:

$$
r+\delta
$$

If the marginal return from health capital is below this cost, the extra health investment is not worthwhile in the pure-investment model.

The optimal health stock **H*** occurs where marginal return and marginal cost balance.

### 12. Comparative statics: age

Ageing raises the depreciation rate of health capital in the lecture.

A higher $\delta$ raises the cost of holding health capital. The optimal health stock therefore falls with age.

This does **not** imply that healthcare use must fall. Older individuals may want a lower optimal stock but may need more healthcare to maintain any given health level. The net effect on healthcare demand is ambiguous.

### 13. Comparative statics: wage

A higher wage makes a healthy day more valuable because productive time can generate more income.

The MEC shifts to the right and optimal health rises. In the lecture, healthcare demand also rises because the individual wants a larger health stock.

### 14. Comparative statics: education

Education improves the efficiency of producing health.

This raises optimal health.

Its effect on healthcare demand is ambiguous: the person wants more health, which can raise input demand, but improved production efficiency means less healthcare may be needed to produce each unit of health.

### 15. Comparative statics: medical-care prices

A lower price of medical care reduces the cost of producing health.

The lecture predicts a higher optimal health stock and higher healthcare demand.

### 16. Health demand and healthcare demand are not the same

This is one of the most important conclusions of Session 3.

The Grossman model separates the desired health stock from the inputs used to produce it. A factor can therefore have a clear effect on **health** but an ambiguous effect on **healthcare use**.

In the lecture:

| Change | Optimal health | Healthcare demand |
|---|---|---|
| Age / depreciation rises | Falls | Ambiguous |
| Wage rises | Rises | Rises |
| Education rises | Rises | Ambiguous |
| Medical-care price falls | Rises | Rises |

### 17. What the model is for

The lecture acknowledges that the idea of people choosing an optimal health path can sound extreme. The purpose of the model is to make the trade-offs explicit and generate predictions that can later be compared with empirical evidence.

Session 4 uses this framework to interpret socioeconomic disparities in health.
