# Session 8 — Insurance III: moral hazard, cost sharing and managed care

## Learning goals

After this session, you should be able to:

- explain why healthcare insurance is more complex than a fixed indemnity contract;
- define moral hazard using expected loss and distinguish prevention effort from loss-reduction effort;
- distinguish ex-ante from ex-post moral hazard;
- explain why full coverage is no longer generally first best once healthcare use responds to insurance;
- identify the two determinants of the extent of moral hazard: price distortion and price responsiveness;
- explain how coinsurance, copayments, deductibles and quantity restrictions reduce moral hazard;
- describe the risk-spreading versus incentive trade-off and why optimal coverage is generally second best;
- interpret deadweight loss from insured overuse in a demand diagram;
- explain how interrelated demand, provider influence and imperfect patient information complicate simple demand-side cost sharing;
- explain the rationale for value-based insurance design;
- compare demand-side and supply-side cost-sharing policies;
- distinguish standard fee-for-service, PPO, POS and HMO organisational structures;
- explain how managed care can affect prices, quantities, quality and technology adoption;
- interpret the empirical findings discussed in the lecture.

## 1. From indemnity to healthcare insurance

A simple indemnity contract pays a predetermined amount when a specified contingency occurs. Under a highly simplified benchmark, an actuarially fair indemnity can produce full insurance and the first best when there is:

- one disease;
- one treatment for that disease;
- a known loss associated with the contingency.

Modern medicine does not fit this simple structure. There are many conditions, many possible procedures and several clinically reasonable treatment paths. The appropriate treatment for the same diagnosis can depend on characteristics that are not fully observable to the insurer before care is delivered.

The lecture defines **appropriate care** as selecting, from the set of efficacious treatments, the option most likely to produce the health outcomes desired by the individual patient.

The insurance contract therefore evolves from paying a fixed loss to covering the uncertain cost of obtaining appropriate treatment after illness. This flexibility creates the possibility that actual healthcare consumption diverges from appropriate care. That divergence is the central moral-hazard problem.

## 2. Expected loss and the two margins of effort

The lecture represents expected loss as:

`E[L] = p(eP) × L(eL)`

where:

- `p(eP)` is the probability that the insured event occurs and depends on prevention effort `eP`;
- `L(eL)` is the size of the loss after the event and depends on loss-reduction effort `eL`.

Insurance can weaken incentives on either margin.

### Prevention effort

Prevention effort changes the probability of the adverse event. Examples include routine monitoring and vaccination.

### Loss-reduction effort

Loss-reduction effort changes the cost of recovery after the adverse event. Examples include choosing a generic rather than a more expensive equivalent drug, or selecting a less intensive treatment when it is clinically adequate.

## 3. Ex-ante and ex-post moral hazard

### Ex-ante moral hazard

Behaviour changes **before** the insured event and makes that event more likely. It operates mainly through `p`.

Examples from the lecture include:

- skipping a flu vaccine;
- neglecting routine blood controls.

### Ex-post moral hazard

Behaviour changes **after** the insured event and makes recovery more expensive. It operates mainly through `L`.

Examples include:

- using an expensive branded treatment rather than a generic equivalent;
- selecting knee replacement rather than pain management when both are feasible treatment paths.

For health economics, ex-post moral hazard is especially important because insurance changes the price of care precisely when the patient is deciding how much treatment to use.

## 4. Why standard fee-for-service insurance creates moral hazard

The standard plan described in the lecture combines three features:

1. providers are paid fee for service;
2. insurance lowers the patient's out-of-pocket price when care is purchased;
3. enrollees can choose providers with substantial discretion.

Once illness occurs, the insured patient faces a lower marginal price and may consume more care. A fee-for-service provider is also paid more when more services are delivered. Patient and provider incentives can therefore reinforce one another.

This is why the optimality of full coverage breaks once healthcare consumption is not fixed.

## 5. The full-insurance diagram

Without insurance, the patient chooses quantity `QU`, where the marginal benefit of the last unit equals its marginal cost.

Under full insurance, the private marginal price falls to zero. The patient's effective demand extends to `QA`, where marginal benefit falls to zero.

The additional units between `QU` and `QA` cost society more than the benefit the patient receives. The resulting triangle is the **deadweight loss** from moral hazard.

The size of the distortion depends on:

1. **price distortion:** how far the patient price is pushed below the marginal cost of care;
2. **price responsiveness:** how strongly healthcare demand changes when the patient price changes.

## 6. Mimicking, severity and the fair premium

Suppose two severity states require different first-best levels of treatment, `S1` and `S2`, with `S2 > S1`.

With full insurance, a patient in the less severe state may prefer the more intensive treatment because the marginal private cost is very low. The patient behaves as if the condition were more severe.

The expected cost of coverage rises from:

`π* = p(L1)S1 + p(L2)S2`

to:

`π′ = [p(L1)+p(L2)]S2`

The premium that is fair after behavioural response is therefore higher than the premium that appeared fair before insurance changed behaviour. This is an example of time inconsistency between the ex-ante contract choice and ex-post utilisation.

## 7. Coinsurance and deadweight loss

Let `k` be the fraction of healthcare expenditure paid by the consumer.

- `k = 1` means the consumer pays the full price.
- `k = 0` means full insurance at the point of use.
- `0 < k < 1` means coinsurance.

If the provider receives price `P`, the patient sees price `kP`. A lower `k` produces a larger price distortion and generally a higher quantity of care.

The deadweight loss is the value of resources used for units whose marginal benefit is below marginal cost. In the lecture's linear diagram, it is the triangle between marginal cost, the patient's price and the demand curve over the excess quantity.

When insurance becomes less generous and `k` rises:

- total healthcare use falls;
- insurer-financed expenditure falls;
- deadweight loss falls;
- the patient bears more financial risk.

## 8. Risk spreading versus incentives

Health insurance transfers resources from states in which income is less valuable to states in which medical spending is needed. This risk spreading is valuable for risk-averse people.

However, subsidising healthcare changes utilisation. Insurers therefore trade off:

- the benefit of smoother financial risk;
- the cost of increased moral hazard.

More complete insurance improves risk protection but worsens incentives. Less complete insurance improves incentives but exposes patients to more out-of-pocket risk.

When moral hazard is important, the **second-best** coverage level is generally less than full.

## 9. Demand-side commitment devices

Ex-ante, enrollees may prefer a contract that prevents their future selves from overusing care and therefore allows a lower premium.

Common demand-side devices are:

| Instrument | Patient payment rule | Main incentive |
|---|---|---|
| Coinsurance | A percentage of the bill | Keeps the marginal patient price proportional to the provider price |
| Copayment | A fixed amount per service | Creates a positive price for each use |
| Deductible | Patient pays the first part of annual spending | Discourages low levels of use and protects mainly against larger losses |
| Quantity restriction | A limit or authorisation requirement | Constrains utilisation directly |

Reducing moral hazard is not free. It can increase:

- the risk of high out-of-pocket spending;
- the risk that useful or optimal care is not received.

## 10. Price distortion and elasticity

A given reduction in the patient price creates a larger utilisation response when demand is more elastic.

Two general results follow from the lecture:

1. services with more inelastic demand can be insured more completely;
2. the optimal coinsurance rate is higher when demand is more elastic.

These results do **not** mean that every elastic service should automatically receive poor coverage. Elasticity may reflect limited patient information rather than low social value.

## 11. Interrelated demand and offset effects

Healthcare services are often related.

An **offset effect** occurs when using one service reduces the need for another. If insured service `A` substitutes for insured service `B`, greater use of `A` can reduce overuse of `B`. This creates an efficiency gain that a single-service demand diagram misses.

The lecture's ordering is:

`c**sub < c* < c**comp`

Interpretation:

- a service that substitutes for other covered care should receive more generous coverage;
- a service that complements other covered care may justify less generous coverage, all else equal.

The same service can be a substitute in one health state and a complement in another. Physical therapy can complement surgery during recovery but substitute for pain medication in chronic musculoskeletal conditions. Dietary counselling can complement diabetes treatment but substitute for bariatric surgery for some patients.

## 12. Limits of the welfare-loss argument

The standard deadweight-loss calculation treats the demand curve as a measure of the patient's marginal benefit.

In healthcare, this assumption is debatable because patients:

- may be poorly informed;
- may not accurately estimate the value of an additional service;
- may be influenced by fear and anxiety;
- often delegate treatment decisions to physicians.

If observed demand does not reveal true marginal health benefit, discouraging care simply because demand is elastic may reduce welfare.

## 13. Value-based insurance design

Preventive care may have highly price-responsive demand. A mechanical elasticity rule would therefore recommend high cost sharing.

But low uptake can reflect underestimation of future benefits. In such cases it can be welfare improving to reduce cost sharing, set it to zero, or even subsidise use.

**Value-Based Insurance Design (VBID)** aligns patient cost sharing with expected clinical value rather than applying the same price rule to every service.

High-value care receives lower patient cost sharing; low-value care receives higher cost sharing or tighter management.

## 14. Demand is jointly shaped by patients and providers

The meaning of demand is less clear in healthcare than in ordinary consumer markets.

Patients may delegate decisions to providers. Physicians may influence demand, including for self-interested reasons. Observed demand elasticity can therefore be jointly determined by patients and doctors.

This weakens the policy conclusion that patient cost sharing alone is the best response to excess utilisation.

## 15. Where is the waste?

### Traditional view

Comprehensively insured patients demand too many services.

Policy response: make users more sensitive to price.

### Alternative view

Waste comes from providers supplying services that have limited value for patients.

Policy response: change provider incentives and make organisations more oriented toward producing health value.

The two views imply different policies, and the lecture uses this contrast to motivate supply-side cost control.

## 16. Supply-side cost sharing and managed care

Managed care emerged as a response to cost growth and inefficiencies associated with standard fee-for-service delivery.

A managed plan integrates:

- the insurer or payer;
- the provider network;
- provider payment arrangements;
- utilisation-management rules.

A typical managed plan restricts provider choice and places some financial risk on providers.

Supply-side controls reduce the patient's direct financial risk, but they can create a **health risk** if useful care is denied or delayed because it is judged insufficiently cost effective.

## 17. Combining demand- and supply-side controls

Suppose there are moderately ill patients `M` and seriously ill patients `S`, but the insurer cannot observe severity perfectly.

A single copayment applied to both types can leave demand too high for both. A single quantity cap can produce the correct total quantity on average while misallocating care:

- `M` may receive too much;
- `S` may receive too little.

Combining a small copayment with a quantity constraint can improve sorting. The copayment discourages lower-value demand by `M` before it discourages the higher-value demand of `S`, allowing the plan to raise the quantity cap for seriously ill patients.

The general lesson is that multidimensional rationing can outperform either price or quantity control alone when optimal treatment is heterogeneous.

## 18. How managed plans contain costs and seek quality

The lecture identifies four major tools:

1. **Selective contracting:** the payer negotiates prices and contracts with selected physicians and hospitals.
2. **Quality assurance:** providers meet accreditation or quality standards.
3. **Utilisation review:** the plan evaluates whether provider practices and proposed services are appropriate.
4. **Gatekeeping and second opinions:** access to specialist or expensive care requires prior clinical authorisation.

Managed care can reduce expenditure through:

- lower negotiated prices;
- lower quantities through utilisation review;
- lower-cost or lower-quality service configurations;
- slower adoption of expensive technologies.

## 19. HMO, PPO, POS and standard FFS

| Plan | Provider network | Gatekeeper | Out-of-network care | Relative restrictiveness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard FFS | No required network | No | Broad choice | Least restrictive |
| PPO | Preferred network | No | Usually covered with higher cost sharing | Moderate |
| POS | Network plus out-of-network tier | Yes | Usually covered less generously | Moderate to high |
| HMO | Care normally through plan network | Yes | Usually very limited except emergencies | Most restrictive |

### HMO

Health Maintenance Organisations provide relatively comprehensive coverage with limited out-of-pocket payments, but generally require use of the network and primary-care authorisation for specialist care.

### PPO

Preferred Provider Organisations offer lower cost sharing within the preferred network and higher cost sharing outside it. They do not normally require a physician gatekeeper.

### POS

Point-of-Service plans combine PPO-style in-network and out-of-network tiers with HMO-style gatekeeping for in-network terms.

## 20. Managed plans as commitment devices

Choosing a managed plan is not only choosing an insurance benefit package. It is choosing:

- a provider network;
- provider payment rules;
- utilisation constraints;
- a degree of freedom to seek additional care.

By enrolling ex ante, consumers precommit to a plan that will restrict some ex-post choices. This can improve expected utility if the lower premium and lower waste outweigh the loss of flexibility.

## 21. Empirical evidence discussed in the lecture

The lecture summarises several findings.

- Cutler and Sheiner use differences in managed-care penetration across US states and report that a 10% increase in HMO enrolment was associated with a 0.5% reduction in the growth of hospital spending.
- A review by Glied reports no clear evidence of lower utilisation rates, but expenditure in managed-care plans was approximately 10–15% lower.
- For acute myocardial infarction, the lecture reports similar treatment patterns and little difference in quality across plan types, while average costs were lower in PPO and HMO plans than in FFS plans. The stated mechanism was lower negotiated price per unit rather than less treatment.
- Higher HMO penetration was associated with slower adoption of some new technologies.

The welfare interpretation is therefore not automatic. Lower spending can reflect lower prices and reduced waste, but slower innovation or lower quality can also reduce future health gains.

## 22. Final synthesis

The managed-care movement shifted competition from a mainly patient-driven model toward payer-driven competition.

Potential gains include:

- lower negotiated prices;
- reduced moral hazard;
- reallocation of surplus from providers toward insurers and insured people;
- stronger incentives to use lower-cost alternatives.

Potential costs include:

- restricted choice;
- denial of valuable care;
- long-run quality concerns;
- slower technology diffusion and innovation.

The policy problem is not to eliminate utilisation. It is to preserve valuable risk protection while discouraging low-value care and protecting access to high-value care.

## Exam focus: high-yield distinctions

| Pair | Correct distinction |
|---|---|
| Adverse selection vs moral hazard | Adverse selection concerns hidden risk **before contracting**; moral hazard concerns behaviour changed **by coverage** |
| Ex-ante vs ex-post moral hazard | Ex ante changes event probability `p`; ex post changes loss size or treatment cost `L` |
| Fair vs full insurance | Fair refers to pricing; full refers to the amount of coverage |
| Coinsurance vs copayment | Coinsurance is a percentage; copayment is a fixed amount per service |
| Demand-side vs supply-side control | Demand-side changes patient incentives; supply-side changes provider/network incentives or quantities |
| HMO vs PPO | HMO uses a network and gatekeeping; PPO uses a preferred network without gatekeeping |
| PPO vs POS | Both can have two tiers; POS adds gatekeeping |
| Risk protection vs incentives | More coverage spreads risk better but usually increases moral hazard |
| Price effect vs selection effect | Moral hazard changes use after coverage; adverse selection changes the risk mix choosing the plan |

## 5-minute review

- Healthcare insurance covers the uncertain cost of appropriate care, but actual use can diverge from appropriate treatment.
- Moral hazard can reduce prevention effort before illness or increase treatment cost after illness.
- Full insurance lowers the private marginal price and can create utilisation beyond the efficient quantity.
- Moral hazard is larger when the insurance price distortion is larger and demand is more price responsive.
- Coinsurance, copayments, deductibles and quantity restrictions are commitment devices, but they expose patients to financial or health risk.
- Optimal coverage balances risk spreading against incentives and is generally less than full when moral hazard is important.
- Interrelated demand, imperfect information and provider influence limit simple demand-side cost-sharing rules.
- VBID lowers cost sharing for high-value care rather than treating all services alike.
- Managed care combines networks, provider payment and utilisation controls; HMO, PPO and POS differ mainly in networks and gatekeeping.
- Evidence in the lecture suggests managed care can lower expenditure, often through negotiated prices, while quality and innovation remain important concerns.
