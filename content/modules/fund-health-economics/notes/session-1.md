## 5-minute review
- Health economics studies choices and resource allocation related to health and healthcare.
- Health can be extremely valuable while the resources used to produce it remain scarce.
- Healthcare expenditure per capita and healthcare expenditure as a share of GDP answer different questions.
- Economic reasoning in the session emphasises trade-offs, opportunity cost, marginal analysis and incentives.
- Healthcare markets combine information asymmetry, uncertainty, externalities and equity concerns.
- Government can act as regulator, insurer and provider.
- Efficiency and equity are separate criteria and both matter in health policy.
- The medical-care triad links patients, providers and insurers/government and previews the rest of the course.
- The lecture uses Beveridge, Bismarck, American and cash-based models as broad teaching categories.

## Notes

### What health economics studies

Health economics applies economic reasoning to the production and allocation of healthcare and to choices that influence individual and population health. It asks how patients, providers, insurers and governments make decisions when resources are limited.

Health itself may be extremely valuable, but producing and protecting health still requires staff, facilities, time, technology and money. Using those resources for one purpose means giving up other possible uses.

### Healthcare expenditure

Two measures are especially useful:

- **Healthcare expenditure per capita:** spending relative to the number of people.
- **Healthcare expenditure as a share of GDP:** spending relative to the size of the whole economy.

The course uses historical international data to show that richer countries generally spend much more per person on healthcare, while countries with similar income levels can still devote different shares of their economies to healthcare.

The lecture links growth in healthcare spending to income growth, technology, ageing, the value placed on life and health, and the expansion of insurance. These trends raise questions about **sustainability** and **equity**.

### Financial intermediation

Healthcare can be financed directly by patients or through third parties.

**Direct payment:** patient → provider.

**Intermediated payment:** patient/taxpayer → insurer or government → provider.

Insurance and public finance can protect people from large uncertain expenses, but they also change the incentives faced by patients and providers.

### Value, average productivity and marginal productivity

The aim of healthcare spending is not spending itself but improved health.

A system can generate large overall health benefits while an additional euro of spending produces only a small extra gain. This is why economists distinguish **average productivity** from **marginal productivity**.

### The method of health economics

Four basic ideas organise much of the course:

1. **Trade-offs:** choosing more of one thing often means accepting less of another.
2. **Opportunity cost:** the true cost of a choice includes the best alternative forgone.
3. **Marginal analysis:** many decisions concern one additional unit, not an all-or-nothing choice.
4. **Incentives:** people and organisations respond to financial and non-financial rewards, costs and rules.

### Why healthcare is different

Economic analysis still applies to healthcare, but several features deserve special attention.

#### Information asymmetry

Patients often need professional advice to know whether care is needed, what their diagnosis is and which treatment is appropriate. Providers therefore have an information advantage, which later becomes important for physician agency.

#### Uncertainty

People do not know exactly how their health will evolve, when care will be needed, which treatment will be appropriate, how much it will cost or what the outcome will be. This uncertainty helps explain the major role of insurance.

#### Externalities

Some health-related choices affect other people as well as the person making the decision. In those cases, private incentives may differ from social costs and benefits.

#### Equity

Societies often care about whether people can access essential healthcare regardless of income or health status. Health policy therefore considers both efficient resource use and fairness in distribution.

### Government in healthcare

The session highlights three government roles:

- **Regulator:** changes rules and incentives.
- **Insurer:** pools and finances financial risk.
- **Provider:** directly supplies healthcare services.

Government may intervene to improve efficiency when markets waste resources and to change distribution when an efficient outcome is considered unfair.

### The medical-care triad

A useful map for the course is:

**Patient ↔ Provider ↔ Insurer/Government**

The patient side leads to healthcare demand, health capital and disparities. The provider side leads to physician behaviour and agency. The insurer/government side leads to insurance demand, adverse selection, moral hazard and public intervention.

### Four broad health-system models

Session 1 uses four simplified models as a teaching framework:

- **Beveridge**
- **Bismarck**
- **American**
- **Cash-based**

These are broad analytical categories, not a claim that every real health system fits perfectly into one box.

### Course connection

Session 1 is a map for the rest of the course. Healthcare demand is developed in Session 2, health capital in Session 3, disparities in Session 4, physician agency in Session 5, and insurance problems in Sessions 6–8.
