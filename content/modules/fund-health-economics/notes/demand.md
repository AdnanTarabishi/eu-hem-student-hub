## 5-minute review
- A demand curve shows quantity demanded at each price; an own-price change causes movement along the curve, while other determinants can shift it.
- Willingness to pay measures how much a buyer values a unit; consumer surplus is willingness to pay minus the price actually paid.
- Healthcare demand is empirically downward sloping, but overall it is relatively inelastic rather than perfectly unresponsive to price.
- Observational estimates can be biased because health risk affects both insurance choice and healthcare use.
- RAND and Oregon use random assignment to study how prices or insurance affect utilization.
- Different categories of care can have different price sensitivity; urgency and necessity matter.
- Arc elasticity compares price responsiveness between two points using midpoint percentage changes.
- The full price of care includes money, travel and the opportunity cost of time.
- Insurance can lower the monetary price while time costs remain, making time relatively more important.
- Session 2 introduces fixed indemnity, coinsurance, deductibles and copayments as different ways insurance changes the price faced by patients.

## Notes

### 1. Demand: slope, shifts and willingness to pay

A demand curve describes the quantity consumers are **willing and able** to buy at each price, holding other determinants constant.

If the service's own price changes, we move along the existing demand curve. If another determinant changes — for example income, health status, or the price of a related service — the entire demand curve can shift.

The lecture highlights several factors that can affect price sensitivity, including the share of income spent on the good, available substitutes, whether care is necessary or more discretionary, and whether the decision is urgent or can be delayed.

### 2. Substitutes, complements and health status

Related services can shift demand.

- If two services are **substitutes**, a higher price of one can increase demand for the other.
- If they are **complements**, a higher price of one can reduce demand for the other.

Health status can affect both the level and shape of demand. When people become sicker, they may demand more healthcare at every price and become less willing to reduce care when price rises.

### 3. Willingness to pay and consumer surplus

**Willingness to pay (WTP)** is the maximum amount a buyer is willing to pay for a unit. It is used as a measure of the value the buyer places on that unit.

**Consumer surplus** is:

$$
\text{Consumer surplus} = \text{WTP} - \text{price paid}
$$

A buyer whose WTP is exactly equal to the market price is the **marginal consumer** and receives zero consumer surplus. Buyers with higher WTP are inframarginal and receive positive surplus.

### 4. Why study healthcare demand?

The lecture gives three practical reasons.

First, insurance changes the price patients face, so insurers and policymakers need to understand how utilization responds to cost-sharing.

Second, differences in access and use across locations and socioeconomic groups create equity questions.

Third, long-term planning for hospitals, medical schools and the healthcare workforce requires forecasts of future demand.

### 5. Is healthcare demand downward sloping?

This is an empirical question.

If patients simply consumed whatever physicians recommended regardless of price, demand might be almost unresponsive to patient price. The evidence used in Session 2 instead supports a downward-sloping demand curve: higher patient prices reduce utilization.

The response is often **inelastic**, meaning the percentage change in quantity is smaller than the percentage change in price, but inelastic is not the same as zero response.

### 6. Why observational estimates can be biased

Suppose we survey people, record their healthcare use and the out-of-pocket price they paid, and then estimate a demand curve.

The problem is that out-of-pocket prices are not necessarily random.

People who expect to need more care may choose more generous insurance. They then face lower prices at the point of use and also consume more care because they are sicker.

If health risk is not fully observed, an analyst may mistakenly interpret a **shift in demand caused by greater need** as a **movement along demand caused by lower price**.

### 7. Why randomized experiments help

A randomized experiment assigns participants to different conditions by chance.

The purpose is to create treatment and control groups that are statistically comparable before the assigned condition. Differences observed afterwards can then be linked more credibly to the assigned price or insurance condition.

Session 2 emphasizes two experiments:

- the **RAND Health Insurance Experiment**, which randomly assigned families to insurance plans with different cost-sharing;
- the **Oregon Medicaid Experiment**, where a lottery created random variation in the opportunity to apply for Medicaid among low-income adults.

The lecture uses both to support the conclusion that healthcare utilization responds to the price patients face.

### 8. Different types of care

The lecture separates healthcare into categories such as outpatient, inpatient and emergency care and also discusses pediatric, mental health, dental care and prescription drugs.

The price response is not identical across all categories. Urgent and necessary care tends to be less price-sensitive than more discretionary care, although the lecture stresses that even urgent care is not necessarily completely price-insensitive.

A key policy concern is that patients may reduce healthcare when prices rise without perfectly distinguishing clinically valuable from low-value care.

### 9. Price elasticity and arc elasticity

Price elasticity measures responsiveness:

$$
\varepsilon = \frac{\%\Delta Q}{\%\Delta P}
$$

For a downward-sloping demand curve, the elasticity is negative.

- $|\varepsilon| < 1$: inelastic
- $|\varepsilon| = 1$: unit elastic
- $|\varepsilon| > 1$: elastic

When comparing two discrete price-quantity points, the lecture introduces **arc elasticity**, using midpoint values:

$$
\varepsilon_{arc}
=
\frac{(Q_2-Q_1)/((Q_1+Q_2)/2)}
{(P_2-P_1)/((P_1+P_2)/2)}
$$

The midpoint formula is symmetric and unit-free.

### 10. The full price of healthcare

The price faced by a patient is broader than the clinic bill.

A useful decomposition is:

$$
P_F = P_M + W
$$

where $P_M$ is the monetary price and $W$ represents other costs such as travel expenses and the opportunity cost of time.

Time can be spent travelling, waiting and receiving care. If insurance reduces the monetary component, time can become a larger share of the full price.

This is why waiting time can act as a rationing mechanism even when the money price paid at the point of service is low.

### 11. Does price affect health outcomes?

The lecture distinguishes **utilization** from **health outcomes**.

RAND found clear utilization responses to cost-sharing, while overall objective health differences were limited, with some effects in particular high-risk groups and selected outcomes.

The Oregon experiment reported improvements in self-reported health and mental-health measures among lottery winners, while the lecture cautions that self-reported health is not the same as objective health and that the experiments differed in population and follow-up.

The main lesson is not that price never affects health, but that the relationship between **price → use** is clearer than the relationship between **price → measured health outcomes**.

### 12. Demand in the context of insurance

Insurance is designed to reduce the patient's exposure to the market price of healthcare at the point of use.

Session 2 introduces four contract forms:

- **Fixed indemnity:** the insurer contributes a fixed amount for a service.
- **Coinsurance:** the patient pays a fraction of the bill.
- **Deductible:** the patient pays an initial amount before reimbursement starts.
- **Copayment:** the patient pays a fixed amount per unit of use.

These designs change the effective price faced by the patient and therefore can change utilization.

### 13. Key policy lesson

Demand theory is essential for understanding both healthcare and insurance markets.

Cost-sharing can reduce utilization. But a lower volume of care is not automatically better: the lecture stresses that patient price responses may not align perfectly with the clinical value of treatment.
