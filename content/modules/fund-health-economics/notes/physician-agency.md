## 5-minute review
- Session 5 asks whether physicians are perfect agents for patients.
- Physician markets differ from simple competitive markets because patients face imperfect information, search and switching costs, price dispersion and insurance.
- The patient is the principal and the physician is the better-informed agent; imperfect loyalty or imperfect knowledge can make agency incomplete.
- Physician-induced demand (PID) is undue physician influence on patient demand that conflicts with the physician's own interpretation of the patient's best interest.
- The benchmark model treats physicians as valuing income and leisure while disliking inducement, so a non-zero inducement level can arise from a trade-off.
- A lower profit rate creates two offsetting forces: the substitution effect reduces inducement, while the income effect can increase it.
- In the McGuire–Pauly synthesis, the direction of the quantity response depends on which of those two effects dominates.
- Evidence on PID is mixed, but the lecture presents inducement as plausibly non-zero and stresses the importance of professional ethics.
- Physician payment methods change incentives: the lecture contrasts fee-for-service, capitation, salary and blended arrangements.
- Small-area variation and practice styles suggest that physicians can be imperfect agents because they may be self-interested, incompletely informed, or both.

## Notes

### 1. Why physician markets need a special model

Session 5 begins from several stylized facts that are difficult to reconcile with a simple competitive-market model.

The lecture highlights:

- imperfect information on the demand side and costly consumer search;
- downward-sloping demand faced by doctors because of switching costs and insurance;
- price dispersion;
- empirical cases where greater physician density is associated with higher fees rather than lower fees;
- settings with administratively set prices where quantity can rise after a price cut.

The point is not that every physician market always behaves this way. It is that these observations motivate a model in which physicians can influence demand.

### 2. The physician as agent

When ill, patients hire physicians.

The **patient is the principal** and the **physician is the agent**.

This relationship differs from an ordinary retail transaction because the physician often knows much more than the patient about diagnosis, treatment options and expected outcomes.

The patient therefore relies on the physician both to recommend and to provide care.

Two problems can follow:

1. the physician may have interests that do not perfectly coincide with the patient's;
2. the physician may also be imperfectly informed.

### 3. What physician-induced demand means

Session 5 defines **physician-induced demand (PID)** as physician influence on a patient's demand for care **against the physician's own interpretation of the patient's best interest**.

That distinction matters.

A physician can influence a patient in a way that supports the patient's interest — for example, encouraging smoking cessation. The lecture treats this as **due influence**, not PID.

PID refers to **undue influence on demand**.

The lecture also distinguishes this from withholding necessary care, which would be an undue influence through supply or rationing rather than through demand.

### 4. Why model inducement?

The lecture gives two broad reasons:

- to explain empirical patterns that standard models struggle to explain;
- to understand how physicians respond to financial incentives and therefore design better pricing rules.

A physician-demand model is therefore both **positive** — explaining behaviour — and a foundation for **normative** policy analysis.

### 5. The benchmark physician model

The benchmark model represents a physician who:

- values net income;
- values leisure;
- dislikes inducing patient demand.

Inducement can raise income, but it creates disutility.

The physician therefore chooses an inducement level by balancing the financial return against the personal or professional cost of inducing care.

The lecture's key intuition is that **some non-zero inducement can emerge as an equilibrium even when physicians dislike inducing**.

### 6. A pure income shock

Suppose physician density increases while fees are fixed.

A physician may experience an income drop.

The lecture uses this to illustrate an **income effect**: depending on preferences and professional ethics, lower income can cause the physician to reconsider the chosen level of inducement.

The size of this response is not assumed to be identical for every physician.

### 7. Lower profitability: two effects at once

A more general change is a lower profit rate per service.

This creates two opposing forces.

#### Substitution effect

Inducement becomes less profitable.

That pushes the physician toward **less inducement**.

#### Income effect

Lower profitability reduces physician income.

That can make additional inducement more attractive as a way to restore income.

That pushes toward **more inducement**.

The net result depends on which effect is stronger.

### 8. The McGuire–Pauly synthesis

The lecture summarizes the benchmark logic through the relative strength of the income and substitution effects.

If the **substitution effect dominates**, lower profitability reduces inducement.

If the **income effect dominates**, lower profitability can increase inducement and quantity.

This framework explains why a price cut can, in some settings, be followed by an increase rather than a decrease in physician service volume.

### 9. Professional ethics

The lecture explicitly includes professional ethics in the inducement story.

If physicians strongly dislike inducing care that they do not believe is in the patient's interest, inducement is more costly to them.

Professional ethics therefore affects how strongly financial incentives translate into behaviour.

This is also why PID should not be interpreted as a claim that all physicians simply maximize income.

### 10. Evidence on PID

The lecture labels the evidence **mixed**.

It discusses studies where fee or income changes are followed by changes in volume or procedure choice in ways consistent with income effects, while other evidence points toward substitution responses.

The lecture also gives an important identification warning:

Empirical PID tests often tell us whether inducement **changes** after an income or fee shock.

They do not necessarily tell us the **absolute baseline level** of inducement before that shock.

The slides nevertheless present a broad view that the equilibrium inducement level is unlikely to be literally zero.

### 11. From positive to normative analysis

The lecture then asks how physicians should be paid.

This is presented as difficult because several forces operate at once:

- physician non-profit motives;
- demand-side moral hazard;
- ability to induce demand;
- quality of care;
- patient selection.

The slide text states a bottom-line result that optimal fees should be below marginal cost and accompanied by a two-part payment scheme.

The available slide text does not contain the full derivation of the intermediate optimal-fee graphs, so the Student Hub does not reconstruct formulas that are not supported by the extracted source.

### 12. Payment methods

Session 5 lists three **pure methods**:

- **Fee-for-service (FFS)**
- **Capitation**
- **Salary**

It also lists **blended methods**, including:

- capitation with fee-for-service carve-outs;
- case rates.

The public-policy discussion treats FFS as a relatively high-powered activity incentive and capitation or salary as lower-powered with respect to service volume.

This does not mean one method is universally best. Different methods create different risks of over-provision, under-provision, quality changes and selection.

### 13. Policy implications if PID matters

The lecture discusses several implications if PID is a dominant force.

A payer may want to:

- control physician-driven expenditure;
- use lower-powered payment incentives;
- recognize that different reimbursement methods can produce too much or too little care.

There is also a deeper welfare question.

If physicians can shift patient demand, the observed demand curve may not be a clean measure of independent patient willingness to pay.

That makes ordinary demand-curve welfare analysis more difficult.

### 14. Practice variation

The second major theme of Session 5 is geographic variation in treatment.

The lecture presents striking historical examples where the probability of surgery differs greatly across nearby areas.

The small-area variation (SAV) literature reaches two broad conclusions in the slides:

- supply variables matter;
- patient demand characteristics also matter, but somewhat less.

However, measured supply and demand factors still leave substantial variation unexplained.

### 15. The grey area of medicine

The lecture emphasizes that many clinical decisions fall into a **grey area**.

There may be:

- limited guidance;
- relatively small marginal harm across several plausible choices;
- benefits that are highly patient-specific.

This creates room for different treatment choices without necessarily implying that a physician is violating scientific evidence or formal guidelines.

### 16. The practice-style hypothesis

The practice-style hypothesis links unexplained variation to physician uncertainty and uneven diffusion of medical information.

Different physicians may develop different beliefs about diagnosis and treatment because knowledge about medical technologies is incomplete and spreads irregularly.

Rapid medical innovation can make this problem larger by continuously changing what physicians need to know.

### 17. What Session 5 ultimately says about agency

The session begins by asking whether physicians are perfect agents.

Its final answer is cautious: **not necessarily**.

Agency can fail for at least two conceptually different reasons:

- the physician may be **self-interested**;
- the physician may be **misinformed or uncertain**.

PID focuses mainly on the first mechanism.

Practice-style variation emphasizes the second.
